[[slnc 500]]
Last week we described discrete random variables: the pmf, p of x equals P of X equals x, the distribution function, F of x equals P of X less than or equal to x, the expected value, the sum of x times p of x, the variance, and five named distributions. From discrete to continuous.
[[slnc 600]]
A discrete random variable takes the values x 1, x 2, and so on, and each value has its own probability, p of x i; they add up to 1. Think of the bars of last week's charts.
[[slnc 500]]
Many quantities in finance are not like that: the time until the next order, the loss on a loan, the return of a stock. They can take any value in an interval. There are so many possible values that we cannot give each of them a positive probability: the probabilities would add up to more than 1. So for such a random variable, P of X equals eh is 0 for every single number eh.
[[slnc 400]]
Why is that reasonable? Pick a number at random between 0 and 1, every part of the interval equally likely. The probability that it lands in the interval from 0.5 minus h to 0.5 plus h is the length of that interval, 2 h. The exact value 0.5 is inside every such interval, so P of X equals 0.5 is at most 2 h for every positive h: it must be 0.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: what is the probability that the next order arrives after exactly 30 seconds, to infinitely many decimals?
[[slnc 800]]
Zero. Between 29.5 and 30.5 seconds has a positive probability; exactly 30 has none.
[[slnc 400]]
So we ask for the probability of an interval, P of eh less than or equal to X less than or equal to b, and the answer is an area: the area under a curve, the density f, between eh and b. That is the picture on the slide.
[[slnc 400]]
Where does the curve come from? Draw last week's bar chart with narrower and narrower bars, and make the area of each bar its probability: the bar height is the probability divided by the bar width. When the bars get very thin, the tops of the bars become a smooth curve. That curve is the density.
[[slnc 1200]]

Continuous random variables and their density.
[[slnc 600]]
X is continuous if there is a function f, its density, such that for every interval, P of eh less than or equal to X less than or equal to b equals the integral of f of x from eh to b. The integral is the area under f between eh and b: this is the picture of the last slide written as a formula.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: P of eh less than or equal to X less than or equal to b equals the integral from eh to b of f of x, d x. f is greater than or equal to 0, and the integral of f over the whole line is 1.
[[slnc 400]]
A density has two properties. It is never negative, since probabilities are never negative. And the total area under it is 1: X surely takes some value, so P of minus infinity less than X less than infinity is 1. These two properties are all we check when we are asked: is this a density? And the second one finds an unknown constant.
[[slnc 500]]
Two consequences. First, P of X equals eh is the integral from eh to eh of f, which is 0: no area above a single point. So for a continuous X it does not matter whether we write less than or less than or equal to: P of eh less than or equal to X less than or equal to b equals P of eh less than X less than b. This is different from last week, where P of X less than 2 and P of X less than or equal to 2 were different for a die.
[[slnc 500]]
Second, f of x is not a probability. It can even be bigger than 1. Tiny example: f of x equals 2 on the interval from 0 to one half, and 0 outside. It is a density: nonnegative, and the area is a rectangle, 2 times one half, which is 1. Yet f of x equals 2. What f tells us is this: for a short interval of length h, P of x less than or equal to X less than or equal to x plus h is approximately f of x times h. So f of x is probability per unit length, like a density in physics is mass per unit volume. Where f is large, values of X are crowded.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: f of x equals c on the interval from 0 to 4, and 0 outside. What is c?
[[slnc 800]]
c equals one quarter: the rectangle has width 4 and must have area 1.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: reading f of eh as P of X equals eh. For a continuous X, P of X equals eh is always 0; f of eh only says how crowded the values near eh are.

Exercise 10.1. Settling a payment.
[[slnc 600]]
The time X, in hours, that a bank needs to settle an incoming international payment is a continuous random variable with density f of x equals c times x times 2 minus x, for x between 0 and 2, and 0 otherwise. Part eh: find the constant c. Part b: find P of X greater than 1. Part c: find P of 0.5 less than X less than 1.5. Part d: what is P of X equals 1?
[[slnc 800]]
Try it in pairs, three minutes. Start with part eh: everything else needs c.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: slide 6 shows the statement; its second page shows the full solution at once, with the graph of f. Ask first, then show, and go through it part by part.
[[slnc 500]]
Part eh.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: which property of a density gives c?
[[slnc 800]]
The total area is 1.
[[slnc 400]]
Show the solution. Outside the interval from 0 to 2 the density is 0, so only that interval counts. Multiply out: x times 2 minus x is 2 x minus x squared. Integrate: the integral from 0 to 2 of c times 2 x minus x squared is c times x squared minus x cubed over 3, evaluated from 0 to 2, which is c times 4 minus 8 thirds, which is four thirds c. This must be 1, so c equals three quarters. Check the other property: between 0 and 2 both x and 2 minus x are nonnegative, so f is nonnegative. The graph is a parabola arch, highest at x equals 1, where f of 1 is three quarters.
[[slnc 500]]
Part b.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: can you find P of X greater than 1 without integrating?
[[slnc 800]]
Yes: the graph is symmetric about x equals 1, so half of the area is on each side.
[[slnc 400]]
The two halves have equal area and add up to 1, so P of X greater than 1 is one half. If someone integrates: three quarters times x squared minus x cubed over 3, from 1 to 2, which is three quarters times four thirds minus two thirds, which is one half, the same.
[[slnc 500]]
Part c. The shaded area, with the same antiderivative: P of 0.5 less than X less than 1.5 equals three quarters times x squared minus x cubed over 3, evaluated from 0.5 to 1.5. At the upper bound, 1.5 squared minus 1.5 cubed over 3 is 2.25 minus 1.125, which is 9 eighths. At the lower bound, 0.25 minus 0.125 over 3 is 5 over 24. So the probability is three quarters times 9 eighths minus 5 over 24, which is three quarters times 22 over 24, which is 11 over 16, that is 0.6875. The payment is settled between half an hour and an hour and a half with probability about 0.69.
[[slnc 500]]
Part d.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: what is P of X equals 1?
[[slnc 800]]
0: no area above a single point.
[[slnc 400]]
P of X equals 1 is the integral from 1 to 1 of f, which is 0. So in part c it does not matter whether we write less than or less than or equal to. And f of 1, which is 0.75, is not a probability: it says that values near 1 hour are the most crowded; P of 1 less than or equal to X less than or equal to 1 plus h is approximately 0.75 times h for a short interval.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: answering part d with f of 1, 0.75; and in part eh, forgetting that the integral runs only over the interval from 0 to 2, where the formula holds.

The distribution function.
[[slnc 600]]
The distribution function is the same as last week, for every random variable: F of x equals P of X less than or equal to x. For a continuous X it is the area under the density to the left of x: F of x is the integral of f of t from minus infinity to x. And the other way round: f of x is F prime of x, the derivative. This is the fundamental theorem of calculus: F is an antiderivative of f. So F and f carry the same information; from either one we get the other.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: F of x is the integral from minus infinity to x of f of t, d t; f equals F prime; P of eh less than X less than or equal to b equals F of b minus F of eh; and P of X greater than eh equals 1 minus F of eh.
[[slnc 400]]
Probabilities from F, exactly as last week: P of eh less than X less than or equal to b equals F of b minus F of eh, because the event X less than or equal to b splits into X less than or equal to eh, and eh less than X less than or equal to b. And P of X greater than eh is 1 minus F of eh, the complement. For a continuous X the endpoints do not matter, so F of b minus F of eh is also P of eh less than or equal to X less than or equal to b.
[[slnc 500]]
The picture: this is the density of the first picture, f of x equals x times e to the minus x, and its distribution function is F of x equals 1 minus 1 plus x, times e to the minus x. With eh equal to 1.5 and b equal to 3.2: F of 1.5 is approximately 0.442, and F of 3.2 is approximately 0.829. So P of 1.5 less than X less than or equal to 3.2 is F of 3.2 minus F of 1.5, approximately 0.829 minus 0.442, which is 0.387. That is the shaded area of the first picture: the area under f becomes the rise of F.
[[slnc 500]]
How does F look? It grows from 0 on the far left to 1 on the far right, it never goes down, and for a continuous X it has no jumps: a jump would be a single value with positive probability. Last week, for a die, F was a step function; now it is a smooth curve. It is steepest where the density is largest.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: a random variable has F of x equals x squared over 4 for x between 0 and 2. What is its density?
[[slnc 800]]
f of x equals F prime of x, which is x over 2, between 0 and 2, and 0 outside.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: and P of X greater than 1?
[[slnc 800]]
1 minus F of 1, which is 1 minus one quarter, three quarters.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: computing P of eh less than X less than or equal to b as F of b minus eh, F of the length. It is the difference F of b minus F of eh, of F at the two endpoints.

Exercise 10.3. Loss given default, a finance example.
[[slnc 600]]
Finance context: when a borrower defaults, that is, stops paying back a loan, the bank usually recovers part of the money, for example by selling the collateral. The loss given default, LGD, is the fraction of the loan that is lost. An LGD of 0 means that everything is recovered, an LGD of 1 means that nothing is.
[[slnc 500]]
The loss given default L of a loan is a random number between 0 and 1 with distribution function F of x equals 0 for x less than 0, x squared for x between 0 and 1, and 1 for x greater than 1. Part eh: find the density f of x of L. Part b: find P of L greater than 0.5. Part c: find P of 0.2 less than L less than or equal to 0.6. Part d: find the median loss: the number m with P of L less than or equal to m equal to one half.
[[slnc 800]]
When a borrower stops paying, the bank usually gets back part of the money, for example by selling the collateral. The loss given default is the fraction of the loan that is lost: 0 means everything is recovered, 1 means nothing is. Here we know its distribution function. Try it in pairs, three minutes.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: slide 8 shows the statement; its second page shows the full solution at once, with the graphs of f and F. Ask first, then show.
[[slnc 500]]
Part eh.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: how do we get the density from F?
[[slnc 800]]
Differentiate: f equals F prime.
[[slnc 400]]
Show the solution. Between 0 and 1, F of x is x squared, so f of x is 2 x; outside the interval from 0 to 1, F is constant, so f is 0. Check: the integral from 0 to 1 of 2 x is x squared from 0 to 1, which is 1. The density grows with x: large losses are more likely than small ones.
[[slnc 500]]
Part b.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: which rule?
[[slnc 800]]
The complement: P of L greater than eh equals 1 minus F of eh.
[[slnc 400]]
P of L greater than 0.5 equals 1 minus F of 0.5, which is 1 minus 0.25, which is 0.75. On the graph: the shaded trapezoid, with parallel sides f of 0.5, which is 1, and f of 1, which is 2, and width 0.5, has area 1 plus 2 over 2, times 0.5, which is 0.75. In three defaults out of four the bank loses more than half of the loan.
[[slnc 500]]
Part c. The difference of F at the two endpoints: P of 0.2 less than L less than or equal to 0.6 equals F of 0.6 minus F of 0.2, which is 0.36 minus 0.04, which is 0.32.
[[slnc 500]]
Part d.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: where does F reach one half?
[[slnc 800]]
Solve m squared equals one half.
[[slnc 400]]
m squared equals one half, so m is the square root of 0.5, approximately 0.707, the positive root, since m is between 0 and 1. In half of the defaults the bank loses at most 70.7 percent of the loan, in the other half more.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: in part b, answering 1 minus 0.5, which is 0.5, as if L were uniform: the distribution function is x squared, not x. In part c, computing F of 0.6 minus 0.2, that is F of 0.4, which is 0.16.
[[slnc 800]]
Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Loss given default. Loss given default, LGD, is the share of the exposure a lender loses when the borrower defaults, after collateral and recoveries; LGD is 1 minus the recovery rate. Typical values: around 0.2 to 0.45 for secured or senior loans, 0.6 to 0.8 for unsecured or subordinated debt. The expected loss is PD times LGD times EAD: the probability of default, the LGD, and the exposure at default; under the Basel rules banks estimate all three. LGD is a random variable between 0 and 1, so it is modeled with a density on the interval from 0 to 1; in practice often a beta distribution, and F of x equals x squared here is a simple special case.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: loss given default, LGD; recovery rate; exposure at default, EAD; expected loss.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: why would large losses be more likely?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: for unsecured loans, recoveries are often small: the bank gets back little. Then the LGD piles up near 1.

Expected value and variance.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you. Clock check: this slide is planned for minute 26 of Session 1, minute 31 when you leave it. If you start it more than about 5 minutes late, in Exercise 10.5, slide 10, give parts b and c as answers only, 1 minus F of 0.8, which is 0.104, and F of 0.4 minus F of 0.2, which is 0.248, and go on to part d.
[[slnc 500]]
The rule of translation from last week: sums become integrals, and p of x becomes f of x, d x. So the expected value of X is the integral over the whole line of x times f of x, d x. Why? Cut the line into short pieces of length h. The piece at x has probability about f of x times h, so the discrete formula gives the sum of x times f of x times h; as h shrinks, this sum becomes the integral. The meaning is the same as last week: the weighted average of the values, the balance point of the area under f.
[[slnc 500]]
For a function of X: the expected value of g of X is the integral of g of x times f of x. Apply g, keep the density. And the expected value of eh X plus b is eh times the expected value of X, plus b: a change of units passes straight through.
[[slnc 500]]
The variance and the standard deviation are defined exactly as last week. The variance of X is the expected value of the square of X minus mu, with mu equal to the expected value of X, and we compute with the shortcut: the expected value of X squared minus mu squared, where the expected value of X squared is the integral of x squared times f of x. The standard deviation, the square root of the variance, is in the units of X. And the variance of eh X plus b is eh squared times the variance of X, and the standard deviation of eh X plus b is the absolute value of eh times the standard deviation of X: a shift does not change the spread.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: the expected value of X is the integral of x f of x; the expected value of X squared is the integral of x squared f of x; and the variance of X is the expected value of X squared minus the square of the expected value of X.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: the example on the slide is f of x equals 2 x between 0 and 1. Before computing: is the expected value of X above or below one half?
[[slnc 800]]
Above: the density grows, so more of the area is on the right.
[[slnc 400]]
The expected value of X is the integral from 0 to 1 of x times 2 x, which is 2 x cubed over 3 from 0 to 1, two thirds. The expected value of X squared is the integral from 0 to 1 of x squared times 2 x, which is x to the fourth over 2 from 0 to 1, one half. So the variance of X is one half minus two thirds squared, which is one half minus 4 ninths, which is 1 over 18, and the standard deviation is the square root of 1 over 18, approximately 0.236. This is the density of the loss given default we just had: the expected loss is two thirds of the loan.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: writing the expected value of X as the integral of x, d x, without the density, or the expected value of X squared as the square of the expected value of X. The density must be in every integral, and the expected value of X squared is a new integral, with x squared.

Exercise 10.5. The recovery rate, a finance example.
[[slnc 600]]
Finance context: when a borrower defaults, the bank rarely loses the whole loan: it sells the collateral, or it gets part of the money back in a court procedure. The recovery rate is the fraction of the defaulted loan that the bank gets back. It is a number between 0, nothing is recovered, and 1, everything is recovered, and before the default it is not known. Banks need its expected value and its spread to set aside enough capital for the losses on their loans.
[[slnc 500]]
The recovery rate R of a defaulted loan is a continuous random variable with density f of x equals 6 x times 1 minus x, for x between 0 and 1, and 0 otherwise. Part eh: find the distribution function F of R. Part b: find P of R greater than 0.8. Part c: find P of 0.2 less than R less than 0.4. Part d: find the expected value, the variance and the standard deviation of R. Part e: a loan of 1 million euros has defaulted, and the bank loses 1 million times 1 minus R euros on it. Find the expected loss and the standard deviation of the loss.
[[slnc 800]]
The recovery rate is the other side of the loss given default: the fraction of a defaulted loan that the bank gets back. Try it in pairs, three minutes: part eh first, then part d.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: slide 10 shows the statement; its second page shows the full solution at once, in two columns: parts eh to c on the left, parts d and e on the right. Ask first, then show.
[[slnc 500]]
Part eh.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: how do we get F from f?
[[slnc 800]]
Integrate from the left up to x.
[[slnc 400]]
Show the solution. The density is a hill on the interval from 0 to 1, zero at both ends and highest at 0.5, symmetric about 0.5. For x between 0 and 1, F of x is the integral from 0 to x of 6 t minus 6 t squared, which is 3 t squared minus 2 t cubed from 0 to x, that is 3 x squared minus 2 x cubed. And F of x is 0 for x less than 0, and 1 for x greater than 1. Check: F of 1 is 3 minus 2, which is 1, and F prime of x is 6 x minus 6 x squared, which is f of x.
[[slnc 500]]
Parts b and c. Now every interval probability is a difference of two values of F. F of 0.8 is 3 times 0.64 minus 2 times 0.512, which is 1.92 minus 1.024, which is 0.896, so P of R greater than 0.8 is 1 minus F of 0.8, which is 0.104. F of 0.4 is 0.48 minus 0.128, which is 0.352, and F of 0.2 is 0.12 minus 0.016, which is 0.104, so P of 0.2 less than R less than 0.4 is F of 0.4 minus F of 0.2, which is 0.352 minus 0.104, which is 0.248.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: why is F of 0.2 equal to 1 minus F of 0.8?
[[slnc 800]]
The density is symmetric about 0.5: the area left of 0.2 equals the area right of 0.8.
[[slnc 500]]
Part d.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: what do you expect for the expected value of R, before integrating?
[[slnc 800]]
One half, by symmetry.
[[slnc 400]]
The expected value of R is the integral from 0 to 1 of x times 6 x times 1 minus x, that is, the integral of 6 x squared minus 6 x cubed, which is 2 x cubed minus 3 x to the fourth over 2, from 0 to 1: 2 minus three halves, which is one half. The expected value of R squared is the integral from 0 to 1 of 6 x cubed minus 6 x to the fourth, which is three halves minus six fifths, which is 3 tenths. So the variance of R is 3 tenths minus one quarter, which is 1 over 20, and the standard deviation of R is the square root of 0.05, approximately 0.2236. On average the bank recovers half of the loan, give or take about 0.22.
[[slnc 500]]
Part e.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: the loss is 1 million times 1 minus R. Do we need new integrals?
[[slnc 800]]
No: it is a linear function eh R plus b, with eh equal to minus 1 million and b equal to 1 million.
[[slnc 400]]
The expected loss is 1 million times 1 minus the expected value of R, which is 500 thousand euros. The standard deviation of the loss is the absolute value of eh times the standard deviation of R: 1 million times 0.2236, approximately 223 thousand 607 euros. The sign of eh does not matter for the spread, and the constant b does not change it. The bank expects to lose half of the loan, but a typical deviation from that is more than 200 thousand euros.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: in part e, the variance of the loss as 1 million times the variance of R: the factor comes in squared, the variance of eh R plus b is eh squared times the variance of R; only the standard deviation scales with the absolute value of eh. Also: reading f of 0.5, which is 1.5, as a probability.
[[slnc 800]]
Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Recovery rates and expected loss. The recovery rate R and the loss given default, 1 minus R, are two sides of the same number. Banks estimate the expected LGD for the expected loss, which is PD times LGD times EAD, and its spread for the capital they hold against unexpected losses. Recovery rates are bounded between 0 and 1 and often have a hump-shaped or U-shaped density; the beta family, with densities proportional to x to the power eh minus 1, times 1 minus x to the power b minus 1, on the interval from 0 to 1, is the standard model. The density 6 x times 1 minus x is the beta density with eh and b both equal to 2.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: recovery rate; expected loss; unexpected loss; beta distribution.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: why do banks need the standard deviation, not just the mean?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: provisions cover the expected loss; capital is held against the deviations from it. The larger the spread, the more capital.

The uniform distribution.
[[slnc 600]]
Now the named continuous distributions. As last week, each comes with a story; if the story fits the problem, the formulas are ready to use.
[[slnc 500]]
The uniform story: a number picked at random from the interval from alpha to beta, with no part of the interval preferred. The density is flat, 1 over beta minus alpha on the interval: a rectangle of width beta minus alpha must have height 1 over beta minus alpha to have area 1. So probabilities are areas of rectangles: P of c less than or equal to X less than or equal to d is d minus c, over beta minus alpha, for an interval inside from alpha to beta: length over total length. Only the length of the interval matters, not where it lies. With c equal to alpha and d equal to x this is F of x equals x minus alpha, over beta minus alpha, a straight line from 0 to 1.
[[slnc 500]]
The mean is the midpoint, alpha plus beta over 2: the rectangle balances there. The variance is beta minus alpha, squared, over 12. Check it on the interval from 0 to 1: the expected value of X is the integral of x from 0 to 1, one half; the expected value of X squared is the integral of x squared, one third; so the variance is one third minus one quarter, which is 1 over 12. On the interval from alpha to beta the interval is stretched by the factor beta minus alpha, and the variance by its square.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: an order is executed at a uniform random time in the next 10 minutes. The probability that it happens between minute 2 and minute 5 is 3 over 10, that is 0.3; on average it happens after 5 minutes, and the variance is 100 over 12, approximately 8.33, so the standard deviation is approximately 2.89 minutes.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: for this example, what is F of x for x between 0 and 10?
[[slnc 800]]
F of x equals x over 10.

The exponential distribution.
[[slnc 600]]
The exponential story: a waiting time, until the next order, the next claim, a default, when the events come at a steady rate lambda per unit of time. The density, lambda times e to the minus lambda x, starts at lambda and decays: short waits are the most likely, long waits are possible but rare.
[[slnc 500]]
Integrate the density: for x greater than or equal to 0, F of x is the integral from 0 to x of lambda e to the minus lambda t, which is minus e to the minus lambda t, from 0 to x, that is 1 minus e to the minus lambda x. And P of X greater than x is e to the minus lambda x. The tail formula, P of X greater than x equals e to the minus lambda x, is the one we use most: the probability of waiting longer than x.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: P of X greater than x equals e to the minus lambda x; the expected value of X is 1 over lambda; the variance of X is 1 over lambda squared.
[[slnc 400]]
The mean is 1 over lambda, by integration by parts in the integral from 0 to infinity of x times lambda e to the minus lambda x. It makes sense: two orders per minute on average means half a minute between orders. The variance is 1 over lambda squared, so the standard deviation equals the mean: waiting times vary a lot.
[[slnc 500]]
The key property: the exponential distribution is memoryless. Having waited s, the probability of waiting t more is the same as at the start. P of X greater than s plus t, given X greater than s, equals P of X greater than s plus t over P of X greater than s, which is e to the minus lambda times s plus t, over e to the minus lambda s, which is e to the minus lambda t, that is P of X greater than t. It is the continuous version of the geometric distribution of last week, which was memoryless in the same way. And it is the partner of the Poisson distribution: if events come at random at rate lambda, the number of events in a period is Poisson, and the waiting time until the next event is exponential with the same lambda.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example: a company defaults at rate lambda equal to 0.1 per year. It survives the next 5 years with probability e to the minus 0.5, approximately 0.607, and the expected time to default is 1 over 0.1, that is 10 years.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: the company has survived 3 years. What is the probability that it survives 5 more?
[[slnc 800]]
Again e to the minus 0.5, approximately 0.607: memoryless.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: mixing up lambda and 1 over lambda. On average one default in 10 years means lambda equals 0.1 per year and an expected value of 10, not lambda equals 10.

Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Order arrivals and memorylessness. Memorylessness says that the market does not remember how long it has been quiet. The normal distribution.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you. Clock check: this slide is planned for minute 57 of Session 1. If you start it more than about 5 minutes late, in Exercise 10.11, slide 17, give part b as the answer only, 1 minus Phi of 1.1, which is 0.1357, and do part d in one sentence: 24.9 percent of 10 thousand euros, 2490 euros.
[[slnc 500]]
The most important continuous distribution: the normal, the bell curve. Its density is f of x equals 1 over sigma times the square root of 2 pi, times e to the minus x minus mu squared, over 2 sigma squared, defined on the whole real line. Two parameters: mu, the expected value, is the center, where the bell is highest, and sigma, the standard deviation, is its width; the curve is concave between mu minus sigma and mu plus sigma and convex outside, so its inflection points are at mu plus or minus sigma. We write X is N of mu, sigma squared, with the variance as the second parameter. The constant in front makes the total area 1.
[[slnc 400]]
Where does it come up? Wherever many small independent effects add up: measurement errors, heights, and, as a first model, the return of an investment over a year. Why sums give a bell curve is next week's topic.
[[slnc 500]]
The problem: e to the minus x squared over 2 has no antiderivative that we can write down, so there is no formula for F. We use one table, for the standard normal Z, which is N of 0, 1, with mu equal to 0 and sigma equal to 1: Phi of z equals P of Z less than or equal to z. Every other normal is reduced to Z.
[[slnc 500]]
How? A linear function of a normal random variable is again normal: if X is N of mu, sigma squared, then eh X plus b is normal with mean eh mu plus b and variance eh squared sigma squared. Take eh equal to 1 over sigma, and b equal to minus mu over sigma: Z equals X minus mu, over sigma, is standard normal. Subtracting mu moves the center to 0, dividing by sigma makes the width 1. This is called standardizing. Then P of X less than or equal to x equals P of X minus mu over sigma less than or equal to x minus mu over sigma, which is Phi of x minus mu, over sigma: subtracting the same number and dividing by the same positive number on both sides does not change the event.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: if X is N of mu, sigma squared, then P of X less than or equal to x equals Phi of x minus mu over sigma; and Phi of minus z equals 1 minus Phi of z.
[[slnc 500]]
The 68, 95, 99.7 rule, from the table: P of mu minus sigma less than X less than mu plus sigma equals Phi of 1 minus Phi of minus 1, which is 2 Phi of 1 minus 1, 0.6826; in the same way 2 Phi of 2 minus 1 is 0.9544, and 2 Phi of 3 minus 1 is 0.9974. So a normal value is within one standard deviation of the mean about two times out of three, within two about 95 percent of the time, and almost never more than three away.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: X is N of 5, 2 squared. Then P of X less than or equal to 7 equals Phi of 7 minus 5 over 2, which is Phi of 1, 0.8413, and P of 3 less than X less than 7 is about 68 percent: one standard deviation on each side.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: for any normal X, what is P of X greater than mu?
[[slnc 800]]
One half: the bell is symmetric about mu.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: dividing by the variance, x minus mu over sigma squared. In N of mu, sigma squared, the second number is sigma squared; standardize with sigma, its square root.
[[slnc 1200]]

Exercise 10.11. The annual return of a fund, a finance example.
[[slnc 600]]
Finance context: the return of an investment over a year is its gain as a fraction of the amount invested: a return of 0.08 means a gain of 8 percent; a negative return is a loss. Before the year starts the return is unknown, and a simple, widely used model takes it to be normal: the expected return mu and the volatility sigma, the standard deviation of the return, describe the investment. Banks and funds report their risk with the Value at Risk, VaR: the 95 percent VaR is the loss that is not exceeded with probability 95 percent. In other words, only in the worst 5 percent of the cases is the loss larger.
[[slnc 500]]

Part d: an investor puts 10 thousand euros into the fund. Find the 95 percent Value at Risk in euros for one year.

The return of an investment over a year is its gain as a fraction of the amount invested: 0.08 is a gain of 8 percent, a negative return is a loss. Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Returns, volatility and Value at Risk. The return over a period is the value at the end minus the value at the start, over the value at the start. Its standard deviation is the volatility; for a broad stock index it is typically around 15 to 20 percent a year. Value at Risk, VaR, at level 95 percent is the loss that is exceeded only with probability 5 percent over the horizon; with a normal return, the VaR is minus the quantity mu minus z 0.95 times sigma, times the value. Banks report VaR, often at 99 percent over 10 days, to regulators. Real returns have heavier tails than the normal model: large losses are more frequent than the bell curve predicts, so the normal VaR tends to understate the risk; Expected Shortfall, the average loss in the worst 5 percent, is the regulatory measure that looks into the tail.

[[slnc 400]]
Answer: with a normal model the worst case is unbounded; any finite loss limit holds only with some probability below 1. The level is a choice of how rare the excluded bad years are.

The distribution of a function of a random variable.
[[slnc 600]]
We know the distribution of X. What is the distribution of Y equals g of X? For example X squared, or the value 10000 times 1 plus X of an investment with return X, or the stock price S 0 times e to the X. The method has three steps, and the first is always the distribution function.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: one, F Y of y equals P of Y less than or equal to y, which is P of g of X less than or equal to y. Two, rewrite g of X less than or equal to y as an event about X, and use the distribution of X. Three, if you need the density: f Y of y equals the derivative of F Y at y.
[[slnc 400]]
Why through F, and not straight through the density? Because F Y of y is a probability, P of Y less than or equal to y, and a probability does not care how we describe an event: Y less than or equal to y, and the equivalent statement about X, are the same event, so they have the same probability. A density is not a probability, and it does not survive such a rewriting; we will see this in the next exercise.
[[slnc 500]]
The example on the slide: X uniform on the interval from 0 to 1, Y equals 2 X plus 3. Then Y is between 3 and 5. For y between 3 and 5: F Y of y equals P of 2 X plus 3 less than or equal to y, which is P of X less than or equal to y minus 3 over 2, which is y minus 3 over 2. Step by step: subtract 3 and divide by 2 on both sides of the inequality; dividing by a positive number keeps its direction. For the uniform X, P of X less than or equal to t is t, for t between 0 and 1. Differentiate: f Y of y is one half on the interval from 3 to 5. So Y is uniform on the interval from 3 to 5: the uniform is stretched to twice the length, and its height halves, so the area stays 1.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: what changes if Y equals minus 2 X plus 3?
[[slnc 800]]
Dividing by minus 2 turns the inequality around: P of minus 2 X plus 3 less than or equal to y is P of X greater than or equal to 3 minus y over 2. Y is uniform on the interval from 1 to 3.
[[slnc 500]]
A linear function keeps the shape of the distribution; this is true for the normal too. If X is normal with mean mu and variance sigma squared, and eh is not 0, then eh X plus b is normal with mean eh mu plus b and variance eh squared sigma squared. The mean and the variance are the familiar rules: the expected value of eh X plus b is eh times the expected value of X, plus b, and the variance of eh X plus b is eh squared times the variance of X. The new fact is that the result is still normal. Why? Take eh positive and use the method: P of eh X plus b less than or equal to y is P of X less than or equal to y minus b over eh, which is Phi of the quantity y minus b over eh, minus mu, all over sigma, which is Phi of y minus the quantity eh mu plus b, over eh sigma. And this is the distribution function of a normal with mean eh mu plus b and standard deviation eh sigma. With eh equal to 1 over sigma and b equal to minus mu over sigma we get standardizing, which we used all through Session 1: X minus mu, over sigma, is standard normal.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example, the fund of Exercise 10.11: the return X is normal with mean 0.08 and standard deviation 0.2, and 10000 euros grow to V equals 10000 times 1 plus X, which is 10000 X plus 10000. So V is normal with mean 10000 times 0.08 plus 10000, which is 10800, and standard deviation 10000 times 0.2, which is 2000. A loss means V less than 10000, that is, X less than 0: P of V less than 10000 is Phi of minus 0.4, which is 1 minus 0.6554, which is 0.3446, the same as P of X less than 0.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: getting f Y by plugging into f X, as in f Y of y equals f X of y minus 3 over 2, which is 1. This density has area 2 on the interval from 3 to 5; the factor one half comes from the derivative of y minus 3 over 2. Always go through F Y.

Part b. Differentiate: f Y of y equals the derivative of F Y, which is 1 over 2 times the square root of y, for y greater than 0 and at most 1, and f Y of y is 0 otherwise. The square root of y is y to the power one half, and its derivative is one half times y to the power minus one half. This density goes to infinity near 0, but its area stays 1: the integral from 0 to 1 of 1 over 2 root y, d y, is the square root of y between 0 and 1, which is 1. A density may be unbounded; it only has to be nonnegative with total area 1.
[[slnc 500]]
Part c.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: look at the two shaded areas. Which is bigger, and why, when the gold interval is not much shorter than the teal one?
[[slnc 800]]
The teal one: the density is much higher near 0.
[[slnc 400]]
Use F Y: P of Y less than or equal to 0.25 equals F Y of 0.25, which is the square root of 0.25, which is 0.5. And P of Y greater than 0.81 equals 1 minus F Y of 0.81, which is 1 minus 0.9, which is 0.1. Check with X directly: Y less than or equal to 0.25 means X less than or equal to 0.5, and Y greater than 0.81 means X greater than 0.9. Half of the probability lands in the first quarter of the interval.
[[slnc 500]]
Part d.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: is Y uniform on the interval from 0 to 1?
[[slnc 800]]
No: the density, 1 over 2 root y, is not constant.
[[slnc 400]]
Squaring pushes numbers between 0 and 1 toward 0, for example 0.5 squared is 0.25 and 0.1 squared is 0.01, so small values of Y are more likely. In the picture the curve is above the dashed uniform line for y less than 0.25, and below it for y greater than 0.25. Squaring does not create or destroy probability; it moves it toward 0.
[[slnc 400]]
Compare with the example Y equals 2 X plus 3 before the exercise: a linear function keeps a uniform uniform; a nonlinear function like x squared changes the shape.

Exercise 10.14. The lognormal stock price, a finance example.
[[slnc 600]]
Finance context: if a stock costs S 0 today and S in a year, its log return over the year is X equals the natural log of S over S 0, so that S equals S 0 times e to the X. Log returns are convenient: the log return over two years is the sum of the two yearly log returns. The standard model of stock prices, which is also behind the Black-Scholes formula for option prices, assumes that the log return is normally distributed. Then the price S equals S 0 times e to the X is said to have a lognormal distribution: its logarithm is normal. Unlike a normal random variable, the price can never become negative.
[[slnc 500]]
A stock costs S 0 equals 100 euros today. Its log return over the next year, X, is normal with mean 0.06 and variance 0.2 squared, and its price in one year is S equals 100 times e to the X. Part eh: find the distribution function F S of s, P of S less than or equal to s, of S, in terms of the standard normal distribution function Phi. Part b: find the probability that the price in one year is below today's price, P of S less than 100. Part c: find the median of S: the price m with P of S less than or equal to m equal to 0.5. Part d: find the price level s with P of S less than or equal to s equal to 0.05, using z 0.95 equals 1.645. Interpret it.
[[slnc 800]]
The log return X is the logarithm of the price ratio, the natural log of S over S 0, so the price is S equals S 0 times e to the X. The standard model takes the log return normal; then the price is called lognormal: its logarithm is normal. Here S equals 100 times e to the X, with X normal with mean 0.06 and standard deviation 0.2. Try parts eh and b in pairs, three minutes: write S less than or equal to s as an event about X.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: slide 23 shows the statement, then parts eh and b on its second page; slide 24 has parts c and d and the density of S. Ask first, then show.
[[slnc 500]]
Part eh.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: how do we turn 100 times e to the X less than or equal to s into an event about X?
[[slnc 800]]
Divide by 100 and take the logarithm: X less than or equal to the natural log of s over 100.
[[slnc 400]]
Show the solution. S is always positive, so F S of s is 0 for s less than or equal to 0. For s greater than 0, the logarithm is increasing, so taking logarithms keeps the inequality: F S of s equals P of 100 e to the X less than or equal to s, which is P of X less than or equal to the natural log of s over 100, which is Phi of the quantity: natural log of s over 100, minus 0.06, over 0.2. The last step is standardizing, as in Session 1: subtract the mean, 0.06, and divide by the standard deviation, 0.2. This is the method of the last two slides; the only new step is the logarithm.
[[slnc 500]]
Part b. A price below 100 means a negative log return: S less than 100 exactly when X is less than the natural log of 1, which is 0. P of S less than 100 equals P of X less than 0, which is Phi of 0 minus 0.06 over 0.2, which is Phi of minus 0.3, which is 1 minus 0.6179, which is 0.3821.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: the expected log return is positive. Why is a fall still so likely?
[[slnc 800]]
The standard deviation, 0.2, is more than three times the mean, 0.06: the mean is only 0.3 standard deviations above 0.
[[slnc 500]]
Part c.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: for which value of z is Phi of z equal to 0.5?
[[slnc 800]]
z equals 0: half of the standard normal lies on each side of 0.
[[slnc 400]]
So the argument of Phi in part eh must be 0: the natural log of m over 100, minus 0.06, over 0.2, equals 0, so the natural log of m over 100 is 0.06, and m equals 100 times e to the 0.06, approximately 106.18. The median of the log return is 0.06, and the increasing function 100 times e to the x carries the median over to the price.
[[slnc 500]]
Part d.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: which z has Phi of z equal to 0.05?
[[slnc 800]]
Minus 1.645: by symmetry, since Phi of 1.645 is 0.95.
[[slnc 400]]
The natural log of s over 100 equals 0.06 minus 1.645 times 0.2, which is minus 0.269, and s equals 100 times e to the minus 0.269, approximately 76.41.
[[slnc 400]]
With probability 95 percent the price stays above 76.41: the loss on one share is at most 100 minus 76.41, which is 23.59 euros. This is the 95 percent Value at Risk of one share over one year, in the lognormal model; compare the normal model of Exercise 10.11.
[[slnc 400]]
In the picture: the price can never go below 0, and the density has a long right tail. That is the advantage of the lognormal model over a normal price, which could become negative.
[[slnc 500]]

[[slnc 800]]
Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Log returns and the lognormal model. Log returns add up over time: the log return over two years is the sum of the two yearly log returns, so a sum of many small independent shocks makes the log return approximately normal; this is the central limit theorem, next week. The price S T equals S 0 times e to the X is then lognormal. This is the model behind the Black-Scholes formula for option prices: there the log return over T years is normal with variance sigma squared times T, and sigma is the volatility. The mean of a lognormal price is S 0 times e to the mu plus sigma squared over 2, here 100 times e to the 0.08, approximately 108.33, above the median 106.18: the right tail pulls the mean up.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: log return; lognormal distribution; volatility; median versus mean; Value at Risk.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: why not model the price itself as normal?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: a normal price can become negative, and a normal model does not compound: the product of two independent yearly growth factors is lognormal if each is lognormal (the log returns add, and a sum of independent normals is normal), but a product of normals is not normal.

Joint distribution: the discrete case.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you. Clock check: this slide is planned for minute 21 of Session 2. If you start it more than about 5 minutes late, in Exercise 10.17, on slide 30, do parts c and d in one sentence each: f X of x equals x plus one half is the inner integral of part eh; and by symmetry, P of X greater than Y is one half.
[[slnc 500]]
If X and Y are discrete, their joint pmf is p of x, y, equal to P of X equals x and Y equals y: the probability that X takes the value x and Y takes the value y. It is a table: one cell for each pair of values, and all cells add up to 1, because exactly one pair of values comes out.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: copy the small table of the slide, without the margins first.
[[slnc 400]]
The distribution of X alone, the marginal pmf p X, is the row sum: p X of x is the sum of p of x, y over all y. The marginal of Y is the column sum. We write the sums in the margins of the table: hence the name.
[[slnc 400]]
Why the row sum? The event X equals 0 splits into disjoint pieces according to the value of Y: X equals 0 and Y equals 0, or X equals 0 and Y equals 1. Disjoint pieces: add their probabilities. That is the addition rule of last week, nothing new.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: in the table, what is P of X equals 0?
[[slnc 800]]
0.3 plus 0.2, which is 0.5, the first row.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: and P of Y equals 1?
[[slnc 800]]
0.2 plus 0.4, which is 0.6, the second column.
[[slnc 400]]
Fill in the margins on the board. Both margins add up to 1.
[[slnc 400]]
The joint distribution function, for any X and Y: F of eh, b equals P of X less than or equal to eh and Y less than or equal to b, both conditions at the same time. From a table: add all cells that are up to eh in X and up to b in Y. Here F of 0, 1 is 0.3 plus 0.2, which is 0.5: the whole first row, since Y less than or equal to 1 always holds. And F of 0, 0 is 0.3: only the top left cell.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: what is F of 1, 1?
[[slnc 800]]
1: every cell has X less than or equal to 1 and Y less than or equal to 1.
[[slnc 400]]
The marginals are in the joint table, but not the other way round: from the margins alone you cannot rebuild the cells. We will see this with two stocks in a few minutes.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, common mistakes: taking the marginal p X of 0 from a single cell, p of 0, 0, which is 0.3, instead of the row sum. And in F of eh, b, using less than instead of less than or equal to.

Exercise 10.15. Two stocks on the same day, a finance example.
[[slnc 600]]
Finance context: the daily price moves of two stocks are usually not unrelated. On a good day for the market most stocks rise, and on a bad day most of them fall. To describe two moves together, we need their joint distribution: the probability of every combination of outcomes, not only the probabilities for each stock on its own.
[[slnc 500]]
On a given day, let X equal 1 if stock A rises, and X equal minus 1 if it falls. Let Y equal 1, 0 or minus 1 if stock B rises, stays unchanged or falls. [[slnc 400]]

Joint distribution: the continuous case.
[[slnc 600]]
Now the same ideas for two continuous random variables: the table becomes a surface, and sums become integrals. A joint density f of x, y, greater than or equal to 0, plays the role of the table. The probability that the point X, Y lands in a region A of the plane is the double integral over A of f of x, y, d x d y, and the total over the whole plane is 1.
[[slnc 400]]
Picture the density as a roof over the square in the picture. In one dimension probability was the area under a curve; now it is the volume under a surface. The probability that X, Y lands in the dark triangle A is the volume of the part of the roof standing above the triangle.
[[slnc 500]]
Marginal densities: integrate the other variable out. f X of x is the integral of f of x, y over all y, from minus infinity to infinity, and f Y of y is the integral of f of x, y over all x. This is the row sum of the table, with an integral in place of the sum: for a fixed x we collect the density over all values of y. And the joint distribution function is the volume over the quarter plane to the lower left of the point eh, b: F of eh, b is the integral for x from minus infinity to eh, of the integral for y from minus infinity to b, of f of x, y, d y d x.
[[slnc 400]]
For a double integral, do the inner integral first, with the other variable held fixed; then the outer one.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: the integral from 0 to 1 of the integral from 0 to 1 of f of x, y, d y d x: inner over y with x fixed, then outer over x.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: f of x, y equals 1 on the unit square, 0 outside. With a constant density 1, volume is height 1 times area, so probabilities are areas. P of X at most one half and Y at most one half, which is F of one half, one half, is the area of the small square, one quarter. The triangle A of the picture, where X is greater than Y, has area one half, so P of X greater than Y is one half. And the marginal is f X of x equals the integral from 0 to 1 of 1 d y, which is 1, on the interval from 0 to 1: X alone is uniform.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: what is P of X equals Y?
[[slnc 800]]
0: the diagonal is a line, it has no area, so there is no volume above it.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, common mistakes: taking the area of a region as its probability when the density is not constant; and in the inner integral, forgetting that the bounds may depend on the outer variable: for the triangle, y runs from 0 to x.

Independent random variables.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you. Clock check: this slide is planned for minute 46 of Session 2. If you start it more than about 5 minutes late, skip Exercise 10.21, slides 36 and 37: say only that they meet with probability 11 over 36, approximately 0.306, and that the solution is in the exercises PDF; then go to the summary.
[[slnc 500]]
Last week, two events were independent if the probability of both is the product of their probabilities. Now we lift this to random variables: X and Y are independent if every event about X is independent of every event about Y: P of X in A and Y in B equals P of X in A times P of Y in B. It is enough to check this for the events X less than or equal to eh and Y less than or equal to b, so an equivalent form is F of eh, b equals F X of eh times F Y of b, for all eh and b.
[[slnc 500]]
In practice we use the two tests on the slide. Discrete: the product rule must hold in every cell of the table; one cell that fails, and they are not independent. Continuous: the joint density is the product of the marginals; on a rectangle this means that f splits into a function of x times a function of y.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example, discrete: the small table of slide 25. The top left cell is p of 0, 0, which is 0.3, but p X of 0 times p Y of 0 is 0.5 times 0.4, which is 0.2. One cell fails: not independent.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example, continuous: f of x, y equals 4 x y on the unit square is 2 x times 2 y, and each factor integrates to 1 on the interval from 0 to 1: independent, with marginals 2 x and 2 y. But f of x, y equals x plus y of the last exercise does not split. Its marginals were x plus one half and y plus one half; at the point 0, 0 their product is one quarter, but f of 0, 0 is 0. Not independent.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: independent X and Y are both uniform on the interval from 0 to 1. What is P of X at most one half and Y at most one half?
[[slnc 800]]
One half times one half, which is one quarter: the probabilities of and multiply.
[[slnc 400]]
In words: knowing the value of X does not change the distribution of Y. Independence is the reason why we can multiply; without it, we need the joint distribution.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: concluding independence from one cell that happens to match. A match in one cell proves nothing; independence needs every cell.

[[slnc 600]]
Correlation and diversification. The table of part b is what the stocks would look like with the same individual behavior but no common driver. The gap between 0.60 and 0.40 is the dependence. In portfolio theory, dependence, measured by correlation, decides how much risk diversification removes: with independent assets, risk shrinks as more are added; with assets that move together, a part of the risk always remains.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: independence; correlation; diversification.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: are stock returns ever independent?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: almost never exactly: most stocks share exposure to the market. Independence is a modeling simplification.

Exercise 10.20. Two borrowers, independent defaults, a finance example.
[[slnc 600]]
Finance context: a bank's credit loss depends on how many of its borrowers default, that is, stop paying their debt, not only on each borrower alone. The simplest model of a portfolio assumes that the default times of the borrowers are independent: one company's default tells us nothing about the other's. Each default time is modeled with an exponential distribution, whose rate is the yearly hazard rate of the company.
[[slnc 500]]
A bank has lent to two companies, A and B. Their default times T A and T B, in years, are independent and exponential with rates 0.02 and 0.03, so their densities are f T A of t equals 0.02 times e to the minus 0.02 t, and f T B of t equals 0.03 times e to the minus 0.03 t, for t greater than or equal to 0. Part eh: find the probability that A survives 10 years, that is, does not default in the first 10 years, and the same for B. Part b: what is the probability that both companies survive 10 years? Part c: what is the probability that at least one of them defaults within 10 years? Part d: what is the probability that both default within 10 years?
[[slnc 800]]
A default time is the time until a company stops paying its debt. We model each with the exponential distribution of Session 1, and the two are independent. Try parts eh to c in pairs, three minutes. Remember the tail of the exponential.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: slide 34 shows the statement, then parts eh and b on its second page; slide 35 has parts c and d and the picture. Ask first, then show.
[[slnc 500]]
Part eh.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: what is P of T greater than t for an exponential T with rate lambda?
[[slnc 800]]
e to the minus lambda t: one minus F of t, which is 1 minus e to the minus lambda t.
[[slnc 400]]
Show the solution. P of T A greater than 10 is e to the minus 0.02 times 10, which is e to the minus 0.2, approximately 0.819. P of T B greater than 10 is e to the minus 0.03 times 10, which is e to the minus 0.3, approximately 0.741. The exponent is rate times time. Company B, with the higher rate, is the riskier one.
[[slnc 500]]
Part b.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: why may we multiply?
[[slnc 800]]
The default times are independent.
[[slnc 400]]
Events about T A and events about T B are independent, so the probability of and is the product: P of T A greater than 10 and T B greater than 10 is P of T A greater than 10 times P of T B greater than 10, which is e to the minus 0.2 times e to the minus 0.3, which is e to the minus 0.5, approximately 0.607. The exponents add, because e to the x times e to the y is e to the x plus y.
[[slnc 500]]
Part c.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: what is the complement of at least one defaults?
[[slnc 800]]
Both survive.
[[slnc 400]]
Go to slide 35. The probability that at least one defaults is 1 minus e to the minus 0.5, approximately 1 minus 0.607, which is 0.393. In the picture: at least one defaults is everything outside the teal region, the three other pieces of the plane together.
[[slnc 500]]
Part d. Each default probability is one minus the survival probability: P of T A at most 10 is 1 minus e to the minus 0.2, approximately 0.181, and P of T B at most 10 is 1 minus e to the minus 0.3, approximately 0.259. By independence the product: P of T A at most 10 and T B at most 10 is approximately 0.181 times 0.259, approximately 0.047. That is the gold square in the picture. A check that ties parts c and d together, by inclusion-exclusion: 0.181 plus 0.259 minus 0.047 is approximately 0.393, the answer of part c.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: is 0.047, under 5 percent, realistic for two borrowers in the same economy?
[[slnc 800]]
Probably too low: in a recession, both get into trouble at the same time.
[[slnc 400]]
In a crisis, defaults come together, so independence underestimates part d, the joint default, and that is exactly the event that causes the largest losses.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: in part c, adding P of T A at most 10 and P of T B at most 10, approximately 0.440, which counts both default twice.
[[slnc 800]]
Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Default times and default correlation. In reduced-form credit models the default time of a company is exponential with a hazard rate, or intensity, lambda: the rate 0.02 means roughly a 2 percent chance of default per year. Survival probability is e to the minus lambda t. The probability that both default is what matters for a lender with exposure to both, and for products such as first-to-default baskets and CDO tranches. Assuming independence understates joint defaults; in 2007 and 2008 this was one reason why such products lost far more than models predicted.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: hazard rate; survival probability; default correlation; first-to-default basket.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: how do practitioners model dependent defaults?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: with common factors or copulas: the marginals stay exponential, but the joint distribution makes defaults cluster.

Exercise 10.21. Meeting for lunch.
[[slnc 600]]
Two colleagues agree to meet for lunch between 12:00 and 13:00. Each of them arrives at a time that is uniformly distributed over the hour, independently of the other. Whoever arrives first waits 10 minutes for the other, then leaves. Let X and Y be their arrival times in minutes after 12:00. Part eh: find the joint density of X and Y. Part b: find the probability that they meet, P of the absolute value of X minus Y at most 10.
[[slnc 800]]
Two independent uniform arrival times: the joint density is the product of the marginals, a constant on a square. Try parts eh and b in pairs, three minutes. For part b, draw the square and the region where they meet.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: slide 36 shows the statement, then part eh on its second page; slide 37 has part b with the picture. Ask first, then show.
[[slnc 500]]
Part eh.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: what is the density of one arrival time?
[[slnc 800]]
1 over 60 on the interval from 0 to 60: uniform, one over the length.
[[slnc 400]]
Show the solution. They are independent, so the joint density is the product: f of x, y equals f X of x times f Y of y, which is 1 over 60 times 1 over 60, which is 1 over 3600, for x and y between 0 and 60, and f of x, y is 0 outside this square. Check: the square has area 3600, so the total is 1.
[[slnc 400]]
A constant joint density on a square: every region gets probability equal to its area divided by 3600. So no integral is needed, only geometry.
[[slnc 500]]
Part b.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: when do they meet, in terms of X and Y?
[[slnc 800]]
When the arrival times are at most 10 minutes apart: the absolute value of X minus Y is at most 10.
[[slnc 400]]
Go to slide 37. That is x minus 10 at most y at most x plus 10: the band around the diagonal, between the lines y equals x minus 10 and y equals x plus 10.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: is it easier to compute the area of the band, or of what is left over?
[[slnc 800]]
What is left over: two right triangles.
[[slnc 400]]
Each corner triangle has legs of length 50, and the two together make one 50 by 50 square. So P of the absolute value of X minus Y at most 10 is 1 minus 50 squared over 60 squared, which is 1 minus 25 over 36, which is 11 over 36, approximately 0.306. Less than one chance in three: a 10-minute wait in a 60-minute window is not much.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, common mistakes: computing only P of X minus Y at most 10, or removing only one of the two triangles. Either colleague can arrive first, so both corners go.

Summary for Session 2.
[[slnc 600]]
Session 2 in a few lines. For a function Y equals g of X: first the distribution function, F Y of y equals P of g of X at most y, rewritten as an event about X; then differentiate. A linear function of a normal is normal, and the exponential of a normal is lognormal. A discrete joint distribution is a table, and the marginals are its row and column sums; a continuous one is a joint density, probabilities are double integrals, and the marginals come from integrating the other variable out. The joint distribution function is F of eh, b, equal to P of X at most eh and Y at most b. Independent means the joint pmf or density is the product of the marginals, so probabilities of and multiply. And the lesson of the two stocks: the marginals alone do not tell how X and Y move together.
