From counting to probability. Slide 4.
[[slnc 600]]
What do we mean when we say that the probability of an event is 0.3? The most intuitive answer: if we repeat the experiment many, many times, the event happens in about 30 percent of the repetitions. A probability is a long-run proportion.
[[slnc 500]]
Proportions behave in a very simple way, and the rules of probability copy that behavior. We write P of E for the probability of an event E. There are only three basic rules, called the axioms.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board, the axioms. First: P of E is between 0 and 1; 0 is less than or equal to P of E, which is less than or equal to 1. Second: P of omega equals 1. Third: if E 1, E 2, and so on, are mutually exclusive, so no two of them can happen together, then P of the union of the E i equals the sum, over i, of P of E i.
[[slnc 500]]
In words: a probability is between 0 and 1; something always happens, so the whole sample space has probability 1; and for events that cannot happen together, the probabilities simply add up.
[[slnc 500]]
Everything else follows from these three. Let me show you the most important consequences, with a tiny example: roll a fair die, so omega is 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and each number has probability one sixth. Let E be the set 2, 4, 6, even, and F the set 4, 5, 6, at least 4.
[[slnc 500]]
The complement rule. E and E complement cannot happen together, and together they make up omega. So P of E plus P of E complement equals P of omega, which is 1. Hence P of E complement equals 1 minus P of E. Example: P of odd equals 1 minus P of even, which is 1 minus one half, which is one half.
[[slnc 500]]
Monotonicity. If E is a subset of F, then F is E plus something else, so P of E is less than or equal to P of F. A bigger event cannot be less likely.
[[slnc 500]]
The difference rule. E splits into two mutually exclusive parts: the part inside F, which is E F, and the part outside F, which is E F complement. So P of E equals P of E F plus P of E F complement, that is, P of E F complement equals P of E minus P of E F. Example: E F is 4 and 6, so P of E F complement is one half minus one third, which is one sixth. Indeed, E F complement contains only the 2.
[[slnc 500]]
The union rule. If we add P of E and P of F, the overlap E F is counted twice, so we subtract it once: P of E union F equals P of E, plus P of F, minus P of E F. Example: P of E union F is one half plus one half minus one third, which is two thirds, and indeed E union F is 2, 4, 5, 6, which has 4 of the 6 outcomes.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: draw the two circles of the slide and label the four regions: E F complement, E F, E complement F, and E complement F complement.
[[slnc 500]]
The Venn diagram is the best way to remember all of this. The four regions are mutually exclusive, and together they make up omega, so their probabilities add up to 1. Every question about two events is a question about these four regions.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: which region is neither E nor F?
[[slnc 800]]
E complement F complement, the part outside both circles.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: in the die example, which outcomes are there?
[[slnc 800]]
Only 1 and 3: odd and less than 4. Probability two sixths.
[[slnc 500]]
For three events, the formula is the same inclusion-exclusion that we used for counts last week: add the singles, subtract the pairs, add back the triple. It is at the bottom of the slide.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: these are the same rules as for counting last week, with probabilities in place of counts. If students ask where the rules come from, point out that all of them are just the third axiom applied to a split of an event into mutually exclusive pieces.

Equally likely outcomes. Slide 7.
[[slnc 600]]
In the last exercise the probabilities were given. Where do probabilities come from? In one important case we can compute them by counting.
[[slnc 500]]
Suppose omega is finite and every outcome is equally likely. If omega has a certain number of outcomes, each has probability 1 over that number, and an event E has probability equal to the size of E over the size of omega: the number of favorable outcomes, divided by the number of all outcomes.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example: a fair die, and E is even: 3 favorable outcomes of 6, so P of E equals 3 over 6, which is one half.
[[slnc 500]]
But careful: equally likely must come from the setup: a fair die, a well-shuffled deck, a person chosen at random. It is not automatic.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: we roll two dice and look at the sum. The possible sums are 2, 3, and so on, up to 12: eleven outcomes. Is the probability of a sum of 7 equal to 1 over 11?
[[slnc 800]]
No. The sums are not equally likely. The 36 ordered pairs are: a sum of 7 comes from 6 of them, so its probability is 6 over 36, which is one sixth, while a sum of 2 comes only from 1 and 1, probability 1 over 36.
[[slnc 500]]
So: choose the sample space so that the outcomes really are equally likely, then count with the methods of last week. And for at least one, remember the complement: P of E equals 1 minus P of E complement.

Conditional probability. Slide 10.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: clock check. This slide is planned for minute 31. If you start it after about minute 35, skip Exercise 9.14, slides 13 and 14.
[[slnc 500]]
Now the most important idea of today. Very often we do not know the outcome, but we know something about it. How does that change the probabilities?
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: I roll a fair die and do not show you the result. What is the probability that it is a 6?
[[slnc 800]]
One sixth.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: now I tell you: the number is even. What is the probability that it is a 6 now?
[[slnc 800]]
One third: only 2, 4 and 6 are still possible, and they are equally likely.
[[slnc 500]]
Knowing that F happened, the outcomes outside F are no longer possible. F becomes the new sample space. Inside F we measure how big the part of E is that is still possible; that part is E F.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board, the definition: if P of F is greater than 0, then P of E given F equals P of E F over P of F. We read it as: the probability of E given F.
[[slnc 500]]
In the example: E is the 6 alone, F is 2, 4, 6, and E F is the 6 alone, so P of E given F equals one sixth over one half, which is one third.
[[slnc 500]]
Look at the picture on the slide: P of E given F is the share of the dark lens E F within the circle F.
[[slnc 500]]
If we multiply the definition by P of F, we get the multiplication rule: P of E F equals P of F times P of E given F.
[[slnc 400]]
It is useful when the conditional probability is what we know. Tiny example: draw two cards without replacement. The probability that both are aces is P of first ace, times P of second ace given first ace, which is 4 over 52 times 3 over 51, which is 1 over 221. After one ace is gone, there are 3 aces left among 51 cards. With three events we keep going: each new factor is conditioned on everything before it. P of E 1 E 2 E 3 equals P of E 1, times P of E 2 given E 1, times P of E 3 given E 1 E 2.
[[slnc 500]]
Last point on the slide: for a fixed condition F, P of anything given F is a probability itself, so all our rules work for it. For example, P of E complement given F equals 1 minus P of E given F.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: stress the order. In P of E given F, the condition, what we know, is on the right, and its probability is in the denominator. Students mix up P of E given F and P of F given E all the time; this comes back in Bayes' formula.

Total probability and Bayes' formula. Slide 15.
[[slnc 600]]
The tree of the last exercise is the key to the next two formulas.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example. On 30 percent of the days it rains. On a rainy day my bus is late with probability 0.5; on a dry day only with probability 0.1. Let F be the event that it rains, and E the event that the bus is late. So P of F equals 0.3, P of E given F equals 0.5, and P of E given F complement equals 0.1.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: the tree of the slide. First F or F complement, then E or E complement, with the numbers of the example.
[[slnc 500]]
First question: what is the probability that the bus is late? The bus can be late on a rainy day or on a dry day: two paths of the tree that end in E, and they cannot happen together. Multiply along each path, then add: P of E equals P of E given F times P of F, plus P of E given F complement times P of F complement, which is 0.5 times 0.3, plus 0.1 times 0.7, that is 0.15 plus 0.07, which is 0.22. This is the law of total probability: split according to F or F complement, multiply along the paths, add the paths that end in E.
[[slnc 500]]
Second question: the bus was late. What is the probability that it rained? Now we know E and ask about F: we need P of F given E, but the data give P of E given F. By the definition, P of F given E equals P of E F over P of E. The numerator is the path rain, then late, 0.15; the denominator is all paths that end in late, 0.22. So P of F given E equals 0.15 over 0.22, which is 15 over 22, approximately 0.68.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board, Bayes' formula: P of F given E equals P of E given F times P of F, divided by the sum: P of E given F times P of F, plus P of E given F complement times P of F complement.
[[slnc 500]]
Bayes' formula turns the condition around. In the tree: one path, divided by all the paths that end in E. Note what happened: rain has probability 0.3, but once we see the late bus, it goes up to about 0.68. The evidence changed our belief.

Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Default, early-warning systems and the base rate. Default means that the borrower does not pay interest or principal as agreed; in bank regulation, typically 90 days past due, or unlikely to pay. The probability of default, P D, over one year is a key number of credit risk. Under the Basel rules banks estimate it for every borrower, and it drives their capital requirements and loan pricing. A 2 percent annual P D is realistic for an average retail or small-business portfolio.
[[slnc 400]]
An early-warning system, E W S, is a model, often a scorecard or a machine-learning classifier on account data, such as late payments, overdraft use, or falling salary inflow, that flags clients before they default. A flag triggers action: a call from the relationship manager, a restructuring of the loan, no new credit lines.
[[slnc 400]]
In statistics language: P of A given D, 0.9, is the sensitivity, also called the hit rate or true positive rate. P of A given D complement, 0.05, is the false positive rate, or false alarm rate. And P of D given A, approximately 0.27, is the precision, or positive predictive value. The 2 percent is the base rate, or prevalence. With a low base rate, precision is low even for a good model: the same effect as in medical screening tests.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: default; probability of default, P D; early-warning system; sensitivity, or hit rate; false positive rate; precision; base rate.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: why not tune the model so that it flags fewer good borrowers?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: one can raise the threshold, but then it also catches fewer defaulters. There is always a trade-off between missed defaults and false alarms; in statistics, this is the R O C curve. Which mix is best depends on the costs: a missed default costs the loan, a false alarm only a phone call.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: if 73 percent of the flags are false, is the model useless?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: no. The bank can afford to look at 6.7 percent of its clients closely, and that group contains 90 percent of the future defaults. Without the model it would have to check everyone.

Odds. Slide 18.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: clock check. This slide is planned for minute 69. If you start it after about minute 74, keep this slide but skip Exercise 9.18, slides 19 and 20; the note there says what to say instead.
[[slnc 500]]
There is another way of describing how likely something is, which you know from betting: odds. The odds of an event H are the probability that it happens divided by the probability that it does not: the odds of H equal P of H over P of H complement, which is P of H over 1 minus P of H.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny examples. If P of H is two thirds, the odds are two thirds over one third, which is 2: 2 to 1 in favor. Two cases where H happens for every one where it does not. If P of H is 0.2, the odds are 0.2 over 0.8, which is one quarter: 4 to 1 against.
[[slnc 500]]
And back: odds o mean o yes cases for every 1 no case, so out of o plus 1 cases, o are yes. So P of H equals o over 1 plus o. Check: odds 2 give two thirds.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: the odds equal P of H over 1 minus P of H, and P of H equals the odds over 1 plus the odds.
[[slnc 500]]
Why are odds useful? Because Bayes' formula becomes very simple. Write Bayes' formula for H and for H complement, given evidence E, and divide the two: the long denominators are the same and cancel. What remains is: P of H given E, over P of H complement given E, equals P of H over P of H complement, times P of E given H, over P of E given H complement. New odds equal old odds times the likelihood ratio: how many times more likely the evidence is if H is true than if it is false.
[[slnc 500]]
Back to the bus. Old odds of rain: 0.3 over 0.7, which is 3 over 7. A late bus is 0.5 over 0.1, that is 5 times as likely on a rainy day. New odds: 3 over 7 times 5, which is 15 over 7, so P of rain given late equals 15 over 15 plus 7, which is 15 over 22. The same as before, with less work.
[[slnc 500]]
So: evidence that is more likely if H is true raises the odds of H; evidence that is less likely if H is true lowers them.

Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Odds, likelihood ratios and credit scorecards. Betting odds first. Bookmakers quote fractional odds, as in the UK: for example, 4 to 1 means a win of 4 per 1 staked, that is, an implied probability of one fifth. Or they quote decimal odds, as in continental Europe: for example, 5.0 means the total payout per unit staked, again an implied probability of one fifth. The implied probabilities of all outcomes add up to slightly more than 1; the excess is the bookmaker's margin.
[[slnc 400]]
Log-odds and scorecards. Taking logarithms turns the odds form of Bayes' formula into a sum: the log-odds after the evidence equal the log-odds before, plus the log of the likelihood ratio. This is why credit scorecards add up points: each characteristic of the client contributes points, and the total score is a linear function of the log-odds of being a good payer. A typical convention: every 20 points double the odds. Logistic regression, the workhorse of credit scoring, models exactly the log-odds.
[[slnc 400]]
Conditional independence. Multiplying the likelihood ratios is correct only if the signals are independent given D and given D complement; this is the naive Bayes assumption. Two models built on the same account data tend to make the same mistakes; then a second flag adds much less information than the factor 18 suggests, and the 87 percent is too optimistic.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: odds, fractional and decimal; implied probability; likelihood ratio; log-odds; scorecard; logistic regression; conditional independence; naive Bayes.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: why do banks use odds instead of probabilities?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: because evidence acts multiplicatively on odds, and additively on log-odds, so independent pieces of information can be combined one by one, in any order. With probabilities you would have to redo the whole Bayes formula every time.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: is it realistic that the two models' errors are independent?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: only roughly, and only if the models use different information; for example, one uses account data and the other market data or the loan officer's judgment. That is why banks like signals from different sources.

The generalized Bayes formula. Slide 24.
[[slnc 600]]
In Session 1 we had two hypotheses: F or F complement. Often there are more: a loan can be in one of three rating classes, an economy can be in boom, normal times or recession. What we need is a partition.
[[slnc 500]]
F 1 up to F n is a partition of omega if the events are mutually exclusive and exactly one of them occurs.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example: roll a die, and let F 1 be 1 and 2, F 2 be 3 and 4, and F 3 be 5 and 6. Whatever we roll, exactly one of these three events occurs. F and F complement form the simplest partition, with n equal to 2.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: is 1, 2, 3; then 3, 4, 5; then 6 alone, a partition?
[[slnc 800]]
No: the first two overlap, a 3 is in both.
[[slnc 500]]
If E is any event, then E is cut into pieces by the partition: E F 1, E F 2, and so on, up to E F n. These pieces are mutually exclusive, so their probabilities add up, and each piece is a path of the tree: P of E F i equals P of E given F i, times P of F i.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: P of E equals the sum, from i equals 1 to n, of P of E given F i, times P of F i. And P of F j given E equals P of E given F j times P of F j, divided by the sum, from i equals 1 to n, of P of E given F i times P of F i.
[[slnc 500]]
The first is the law of total probability, the second the generalized Bayes formula. In words, exactly as before: add the paths that end in E; and Bayes is one path divided by all of them. The only change is that the tree now has n branches instead of 2.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example, on the board. We have three boxes. Box 1 contains only red balls, box 2 is half red and half blue, box 3 contains only blue balls. We pick a box at random and draw a ball; let R be the event that the ball is red. P of R equals one third times 1, plus one third times one half, plus one third times 0, which is one half. The ball is red. Which box did it come from? P of box 1 given R equals one third over one half, which is two thirds. P of box 2 given R equals one sixth over one half, which is one third. And P of box 3 given R is 0.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: why is box 1 now twice as likely as box 2?
[[slnc 800]]
Box 1 produces red balls twice as often; before the draw, all three boxes were equally likely.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: point at the tree on the slide: one path to E for each F i. The posterior probabilities P of F j given E always add up to 1; tell them to use this as a check.

Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Internal ratings and probabilities of default. An internal rating is a bank's own grade for every borrower, based on financial statements, payment history, industry, and so on. For every grade the bank estimates a probability of default, P D, the chance that the borrower stops paying within one year. These are exactly the numbers P of D given A, P of D given B and P of D given C of the exercise. The portfolio default rate, P of D equals 0.03, is the P D of a randomly chosen loan: a weighted average of the class P D's.
[[slnc 400]]
Why the Bayes direction matters. The P D's answer the question: how risky is this class? Bayes answers the opposite question: where do our defaults come from? This is important for the bank's credit committee, to see whether the loss is concentrated in one class, and for checking the rating system: a good rating system puts most of the later defaults in the worst classes. Here class C gives 8 over 15, approximately 53 percent, of the defaults, so the ratings clearly separate good and bad borrowers.
[[slnc 400]]
What the P D's are used for. The expected loss of a loan, E L, equals P D times L G D times E A D: the probability of default, times the loss given default, which is the share that is not recovered, times the exposure at default, which is the amount owed. The bank prices this into the interest rate; under the Basel rules the P D's also determine how much capital it must hold against unexpected losses.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: internal rating; probability of default, P D; loss given default, L G D; exposure at default, E A D; expected loss; Basel capital rules.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: is a 3 percent default rate realistic?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: for a portfolio of loans to smaller, riskier companies, yes; for large, highly rated companies the one-year default rates are far below 1 percent. Default rates also rise sharply in recessions, so a bank's P D's are estimated over a whole economic cycle.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: if C loans are so bad, why does the bank lend to them at all?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: because it charges them a higher interest rate. If the extra interest covers the expected loss and the cost of capital, the C loans can even be the most profitable part of the portfolio.

Independent events. Slide 28.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: clock check. This slide is planned for minute 19. If you start it after about minute 24, skip Exercise 9.26, slides 29 and 30.
[[slnc 500]]
Usually, knowing that F has happened changes the probability of E: that was the whole point of conditional probability. Sometimes, however, it does not change anything. Then we call the events independent.
[[slnc 500]]
E and F are independent if P of E F equals P of E times P of F.
[[slnc 500]]
Why this formula? If P of F is greater than 0, divide both sides by P of F: P of E given F, which is P of E F over P of F, equals P of E. So knowing F does not change the chances of E. We use the product form as the definition because it is symmetric in E and F, and it also works when P of F equals 0.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example. Roll a fair die. Let E be 2, 4, 6, even, and F be 1 and 2, at most 2. Then E F is just the 2, and P of E F is one sixth, while P of E times P of F is one half times one third, which is one sixth. Equal: E and F are independent. Indeed, among 1 and 2 half of the numbers are even, just as among all six numbers.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: and with G equal to 1, 2, 3, at most 3?
[[slnc 800]]
E G is just the 2, and P of E G is one sixth, but P of E times P of G is one half times one half, which is one quarter: not independent. Among 1, 2, 3 only one number is even.
[[slnc 500]]
Independence is a property of the numbers, not of the story: you check the equation.
[[slnc 500]]
If E and F are independent, then E and F complement are independent too: if knowing that F happened tells us nothing about E, then knowing that F did not happen tells us nothing either.
[[slnc 500]]
Three events E, F, G are independent if all four product rules hold: P of E F equals P of E times P of F; P of E G equals P of E times P of G; P of F G equals P of F times P of G; and P of E F G equals P of E times P of F times P of G. Doesn't the last one follow from the first three? No: the next exercise gives an example where it fails.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: two events are mutually exclusive, each with positive probability. Can they be independent?
[[slnc 800]]
No: if one occurs, the other cannot, so P of E F is 0, while P of E times P of F is greater than 0.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: students very often confuse mutually exclusive with independent. Say it explicitly: mutually exclusive events are strongly dependent; knowing one tells you the other did not happen.
[[slnc 500]]
The most useful consequence: n independent trials, each a success with probability p. At least one success is the complement of no success at all, and for independent trials we multiply: P of at least one success equals 1 minus the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n. For example, three tosses of a fair coin: P of at least one head equals 1 minus one half cubed, which is seven eighths.

Exercise 9.26. Pairwise but not mutually independent. Slides 29 and 30.
[[slnc 600]]
Two fair coins are tossed. Let A be the event that the first coin shows heads, B the event that the second coin shows heads, and C the event that the two coins show the same side. Part a: are A and B independent? A and C? B and C? Part b: are A, B, C mutually independent? Part c: explain in words why knowing two of the events tells you everything about the third.
[[slnc 800]]
Note for you: short on time? Skip this exercise, 7 minutes: go from slide 28 straight to slide 31. Say: for three events, pairwise independence is not enough. You need the product rule for every group, including all three together. Exercise 9.26 in the exercises PDF shows an example.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: give them two minutes for part a: list omega and the three events as sets, then check the three product rules. Most students guess that A and C are dependent; let them find out that they are not.
[[slnc 500]]
Four equally likely outcomes, each with probability one quarter: omega consists of heads heads, heads tails, tails heads, and tails tails. The first letter is the first coin.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: write A, B and C as sets.
[[slnc 800]]
A is heads heads and heads tails. B is heads heads and tails heads. C is heads heads and tails tails.
[[slnc 500]]
Each contains two of the four outcomes, so P of A, P of B and P of C are all one half.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: the table from the slide. The rows are the outcomes, and a dot means that the event of the column occurs. Heads heads has a dot under A, B and C. Heads tails has a dot only under A. Tails heads only under B. Tails tails only under C.
[[slnc 500]]
Part a. Look at the table: the only outcome that is in two columns at once is heads heads. So every pair has the same intersection: A B, A C and B C are all just heads heads, with probability one quarter. And every product of two probabilities is one half times one half, which is one quarter. So P of A B equals P of A times P of B, P of A C equals P of A times P of C, and P of B C equals P of B times P of C. Every pair is independent: the events are pairwise independent.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: A and B are clearly independent: two separate coins. But why are A and C independent?
[[slnc 800]]
If the first coin is heads, same side means the second coin is heads too, which still has probability one half: P of C given A is one half, which is P of C.
[[slnc 500]]
Part b. Next slide. For three events we need all four equations. The first three hold by part a. The fourth: all three events occur only at heads heads, so A B C is just heads heads, and P of A B C is one quarter, but P of A times P of B times P of C is one half times one half times one half, which is one eighth. One quarter is not equal to one eighth, so A, B, C are not independent.
[[slnc 500]]
Part c. If A and B both occur, the outcome is heads heads, so C occurs for sure: P of C given A B equals 1, which is not equal to one half, the value of P of C. One coin alone says nothing about same side; two of the events together decide the third.
[[slnc 500]]
The moral: pairwise independence is not enough. For three events, check all four equations.

Exercise 9.27. Bond defaults: independent or not? A finance example. Slides 31 to 33.
[[slnc 600]]
Two bonds; D i is the event that bond i defaults within a year, and P of D 1 equals P of D 2 equals 0.05. Find the probability that at least one defaults, which is when a first-to-default swap pays, and the probability that both default, which is when a second-to-default swap pays: a, if the defaults are independent; b, if P of D 1 D 2 equals 0.02, because the issuers depend on the same economy. Part c: in case b, what is P of D 2 given D 1? Compare it with P of D 2. Part d: 10 bonds default independently, each with probability 0.05. What is the probability of at least one default?
[[slnc 800]]
Note for you: give them three minutes for parts a and b. Before they start, make sure they translate the two swaps into events; this is the first reveal of the slide.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: which event makes the first-to-default swap pay, and which the second-to-default swap?
[[slnc 800]]
At least one default: D 1 union D 2. Both default: D 1 D 2.
[[slnc 500]]
For the union we always have inclusion-exclusion: P of D 1 union D 2 equals P of D 1, plus P of D 2, minus P of D 1 D 2. So in every case the key number is P of D 1 D 2.
[[slnc 500]]
Part a. Independent: the probability of both is the product. P of D 1 D 2 equals P of D 1 times P of D 2, which is 0.05 times 0.05, which is 0.0025. And P of D 1 union D 2 equals 0.05 plus 0.05 minus 0.0025, which is 0.0975.
[[slnc 500]]
Check with the complement. No default is D 1 complement D 2 complement, by De Morgan, and the complements of independent events are independent, each with probability 0.95. So P of D 1 union D 2 equals 1 minus 0.95 squared, which is 1 minus 0.9025, which is 0.0975.
[[slnc 500]]
Part b. Next slide. Now the two issuers depend on the same economy, and P of D 1 D 2 equals 0.02 is given.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: can we multiply 0.05 times 0.05 here?
[[slnc 800]]
No: the product rule holds only for independent events.
[[slnc 500]]
0.02 is 8 times as large as 0.0025. The union is again inclusion-exclusion: P of D 1 union D 2 equals 0.05 plus 0.05 minus 0.02, which is 0.08.
[[slnc 500]]
Part c. The definition of conditional probability: P of D 2 given D 1 equals P of D 1 D 2 over P of D 1, which is 0.02 over 0.05, which is 0.4. 0.4 is not equal to 0.05, the value of P of D 2: the defaults are not independent. Once bond 1 has defaulted, probably because the economy is in trouble, bond 2 defaults with probability 0.4 instead of 0.05. This is positive dependence.
[[slnc 500]]
Part d. Last slide. Back to independence, but now with 10 bonds.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: how would you compute at least one default for 10 bonds?
[[slnc 800]]
Not with inclusion-exclusion: far too many terms. Complement: at least one default is the opposite of all 10 bonds survive.
[[slnc 500]]
Each bond survives with probability 0.95; the bonds are independent, so we multiply. P of no default equals 0.95 to the power 10, approximately 0.599, and P of at least one default equals 1 minus 0.95 to the power 10, approximately 0.401. This is the formula 1 minus the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n, from the theory slide.
[[slnc 500]]
Each bond is fairly safe, but with 10 of them a first-to-default swap pays with probability about 40 percent.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board, the comparison table for two bonds. Independent: at least one, 0.0975; both, 0.0025. Same economy: at least one, 0.08; both, 0.02.
[[slnc 500]]
Lesson: positive dependence makes at least one slightly less likely, but both much more likely. The defaults come together, so they overlap more.
[[slnc 800]]
Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Default correlation and basket swaps. First-to-default and second-to-default swaps are insurance contracts on a basket of bonds. The protection buyer pays a regular premium, called the spread; the first-to-default swap pays at the first default in the basket, the second-to-default swap only at the second. In this exercise the first-to-default swap pays on D 1 union D 2, and the second-to-default swap on D 1 D 2.
[[slnc 400]]
Default correlation. Companies depend on the same economy: in a recession many of them get into trouble at the same time. This common, or systematic, factor makes defaults positively dependent. One measures it by the correlation of the default indicators: rho equals P of D 1 D 2 minus P of D 1 times P of D 2, divided by the square root of the product P of D 1 times 1 minus P of D 1, times P of D 2 times 1 minus P of D 2. In case b this is 0.02 minus 0.0025, over 0.05 times 0.95, approximately 0.37; under independence rho equals 0. Students will meet correlation later; you do not need it in class.
[[slnc 400]]
Why it matters for prices. The table of the last slide is the whole story: with more correlation the second-to-default event becomes much more likely, so its seller wants a higher spread, while the first-to-default event becomes slightly less likely. Banks model this with factor models, for example the Gaussian copula. In 2008 the correlation of mortgage defaults was badly underestimated: products that only lose after many defaults, the senior tranches of mortgage C D O's, lost much more than the models predicted.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: first-to-default and second-to-default swap; spread; default correlation; systematic risk; factor model; Gaussian copula.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: where would a number like P of D 1 D 2 equals 0.02 come from?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: from history, that is, how often similar companies defaulted in the same year, or from market prices of basket products, from which the correlation the market assumes can be backed out. Either way it is an estimate, and a very uncertain one.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: is a diversified bond portfolio then safe?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: diversification removes the company-specific part of the risk, but not the common factor. In a deep recession many bonds default together, whatever the number of bonds.

Discrete random variables. Slide 34.
[[slnc 600]]
Until now we asked whether an event happens. In finance we usually ask how much: how much do we win, how much does the portfolio lose. For that we need random variables.
[[slnc 500]]
A random variable X assigns a number to every outcome. It is discrete if it takes finitely or countably many values.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example. Toss two fair coins, so omega consists of heads heads, heads tails, tails heads, and tails tails, and let X be the number of heads. Then X of heads heads is 2, X of heads tails and X of tails heads are 1, and X of tails tails is 0. So X is not random in a mysterious way: it is just a function on omega, and the randomness comes from the outcome.
[[slnc 500]]
The probability mass function, pmf for short, gives the probability of each value: p of x equals P of X equals x. It is never negative, and the values add up to 1.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board, for the two coins: p of 0 is one quarter, p of 1 is one half, and p of 2 is one quarter.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: why is p of 1 one half?
[[slnc 800]]
Two outcomes, heads tails and tails heads, give one head: two quarters.
[[slnc 500]]
The distribution function adds up the pmf from the left: F of x equals P of X less than or equal to x, which is the sum of p of y over all y less than or equal to x. For the two coins: F of x is 0 for x less than 0, then one quarter from 0, then one quarter plus one half, which is three quarters, from 1, and 1 from 2 on.
[[slnc 500]]
So F is a step function. It jumps by p of x at every value x, it is constant in between, and it rises from 0 to 1.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: point at the graph on the slide. There p of 1 is one quarter, p of 2 is one half, and p of 3 and p of 4 are one eighth each. Stress the dots: at a jump the filled dot is on the upper step, because F of x, which is P of X less than or equal to x, already includes p of x. This is the most common mistake in the sketch of the urn exercise.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: on the slide's graph, what is F of 2, and what is F of 2.5?
[[slnc 800]]
Both are three quarters: F of 2 includes p of 2, and there is no value between 2 and 2.5.
[[slnc 500]]
From F we can read off interval probabilities: P of a less than X less than or equal to b equals F of b minus F of a, and P of X greater than a equals 1 minus F of a. For example, on the slide, P of 1 less than X less than or equal to 3 equals F of 3 minus F of 1, which is seven eighths minus one quarter, which is five eighths, and that is p of 2 plus p of 3, one half plus one eighth. Watch the inequalities: a itself is not included, b is.

Exercise 9.32. A game with an urn. Slides 35 to 37.
[[slnc 600]]
Two balls are drawn without replacement from an urn with 8 white, 4 black and 2 orange balls. Each black ball wins 2 euros, each white ball loses 1 euro, and an orange ball gives nothing. X is the net winnings. Find: a, the pmf of X; b, the distribution function F of x, which is P of X less than or equal to x, with a sketch; c, P of X greater than 0, and F of 1; d, E of X, the variance of X, and the standard deviation of X.
[[slnc 800]]
Note for you: the longest exercise of the day. Let them work in pairs on part a for four minutes. Hint if they are stuck: list the possible color pairs first. Then go through the slides reveal by reveal.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: how many balls are in the urn, and how many pairs can we draw?
[[slnc 800]]
8 plus 4 plus 2, which is 14 balls; the order does not matter, so 14 choose 2, which is 91 pairs.
[[slnc 500]]
Part a. All 91 pairs are equally likely. We list the possible pairs by their colors and count each kind.
[[slnc 400]]
Two white: 8 choose 2, which is 28. White and orange: 8 times 2, which is 16; one of the 8 white balls and one of the 2 orange ones. Two orange: 1. White and black: 8 times 4, which is 32. Black and orange: 4 times 2, which is 8. Two black: 4 choose 2, which is 6.

[[slnc 500]]

Part b. Next slide. Add up the pmf from the left: 28; 28 plus 16, which is 44; 44 plus 1, which is 45; 45 plus 32, which is 77; 77 plus 8, which is 85; 85 plus 6, which is 91; all over 91.
[[slnc 400]]

It is 28 over 91 from minus 2 up to, but not including, minus 1. 44 over 91 from minus 1 up to 0. 45 over 91 from 0 up to 1. 77 over 91 from 1 up to 2. 85 over 91 from 2 up to 4. [[slnc 800]]
28 over 91: X less than or equal to minus 2 includes X equal to minus 2.

Expected value, variance, standard deviation. Slide 38.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: clock check. This slide is planned for minute 59. If you start it after about minute 64, skip Exercise 9.37, slides 41 and 42, after the call option.
[[slnc 500]]
The expected value of X is the average of its values, weighted by their probabilities: E of X equals the sum, over all values x, of x times p of x. Think of it as the long-run average: if we play the game many times, the average winnings per game get close to E of X.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example, the two coins again: X is the number of heads, with probabilities one quarter, one half and one quarter for 0, 1 and 2. E of X equals 0 times one quarter, plus 1 times one half, plus 2 times one quarter, which is 1.
[[slnc 500]]
A function of X: apply the function to every value, keep the probabilities. E of g of X equals the sum, over all values x, of g of x times p of x. For example, E of X squared equals 0 times one quarter, plus 1 times one half, plus 4 times one quarter, which is 3 over 2. Note: 3 over 2 is not the square of E of X, which is 1. In general E of g of X is not g of E of X; we see a finance example of this in a minute.
[[slnc 500]]
There is one important exception: linear functions. E of a X plus b equals a times E of X, plus b. If every head pays 10 euros and the game costs 5 euros, the net gain is 10 X minus 5, and its expected value is 10 times 1 minus 5, which is 5 euros.
[[slnc 500]]
The expected value tells us the center; it says nothing about the spread. The variance is the expected squared distance from the mean, mu, which is E of X: the variance of X equals E of the quantity X minus mu, squared, which equals E of X squared minus the square of E of X.
[[slnc 500]]
For the coins, from the definition: 0 minus 1, squared, times one quarter, plus 1 minus 1, squared, times one half, plus 2 minus 1, squared, times one quarter, which is one half. With the shortcut: 3 over 2 minus 1 squared, which is one half. The same number; the shortcut is usually faster.
[[slnc 500]]
The variance is in squared units, squared euros, so we take the square root: the standard deviation of X is the square root of the variance of X. For the coins the standard deviation of X is the square root of one half, approximately 0.707.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: the variance of a X plus b equals a squared times the variance of X, and the standard deviation of a X plus b equals the absolute value of a, times the standard deviation of X.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: why does b disappear?
[[slnc 800]]
Adding a constant shifts every value and the mean by the same amount; the distances from the mean do not change.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: why a squared?
[[slnc 800]]
Every distance from the mean is multiplied by a, and the variance squares the distances.
[[slnc 500]]
So for 10 X minus 5, the standard deviation is 10 times 0.707, approximately 7.07 euros.
[[slnc 500]]
In finance: E of X is the expected return or payoff, and the standard deviation of X measures the risk.

Exercise 9.36. The payoff of a call option, a finance example. Slides 39 and 40.
[[slnc 600]]
The price of a stock in one year, S, is 80, 100, 120 or 140, with probabilities 0.2, 0.4, 0.3 and 0.1. Part a: find E of S, the variance of S, and the standard deviation of S. Part b: a call option with strike price 110 pays C, the maximum of S minus 110, and 0, in one year. Find the pmf of C, E of C, and the standard deviation of C. Part c: compare E of C with the maximum of E of S minus 110, and 0.
[[slnc 800]]
Note for you: explain the call option in two sentences before they start; that is the first paragraph below. Then give them three minutes for part a.
[[slnc 500]]
A quick word on the finance. A call option with strike price 110 gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy the stock for 110 in one year. If the stock then costs more than 110, you buy at 110 and gain the difference; if it costs less, you simply do not use the option. So the payoff is C equals the maximum of S minus 110, and 0: never negative.
[[slnc 500]]
Part a. Each price times its probability, then add. E of S equals 80 times 0.2, plus 100 times 0.4, plus 120 times 0.3, plus 140 times 0.1, which is 16 plus 40 plus 36 plus 14, which is 106.
[[slnc 400]]
For the variance, first the mean of S squared: E of S squared equals 6,400 times 0.2, plus 10,000 times 0.4, plus 14,400 times 0.3, plus 19,600 times 0.1, which is 1,280 plus 4,000 plus 4,320 plus 1,960, which is 11,560. The variance of S equals E of S squared minus the square of E of S, which is 11,560 minus 106 squared, that is 11,560 minus 11,236, which is 324. And the standard deviation of S is the square root of 324, which is 18. The price is typically about 18 away from 106.
[[slnc 500]]
Part b. Next slide. C is a function of S: compute the payoff for each price.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: what does the option pay if S is 80? 100? 120? 140?
[[slnc 800]]
0, 0, 10 and 30.
[[slnc 500]]
Two prices give C equals 0, so their probabilities add up: p C of 0 is 0.2 plus 0.4, which is 0.6; p C of 10 is 0.3; and p C of 30 is 0.1. E of C equals 0 times 0.6, plus 10 times 0.3, plus 30 times 0.1, which is 3 plus 3, which is 6. E of C squared equals 0, plus 100 times 0.3, plus 900 times 0.1, which is 120. The variance of C is 120 minus 6 squared, which is 84, and the standard deviation of C is the square root of 84, approximately 9.17.
[[slnc 500]]
Part c.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: what do we get if we put the expected price into the payoff?
[[slnc 800]]
The maximum of 106 minus 110, and 0, which is 0.
[[slnc 500]]
But E of C is 6. The expected price is below the strike, so plugging in the average says the option is worthless. But the price is random: with probability 0.4 it ends above 110, and then the option pays; otherwise it pays 0, never a negative amount. So the average payoff is positive.
[[slnc 500]]
In general, E of g of X is not equal to g of E of X. Apply g first, then average: E of g of X equals the sum, over all values x, of g of x times p of x.
[[slnc 800]]
Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
Call options and why uncertainty has value. A call option is the right, but not the obligation, to buy an asset, called the underlying, at the strike price K on the expiry date. Its payoff, the maximum of S minus K, and 0, has the famous hockey stick graph: zero below K, then rising one-for-one. A put is the right to sell, with payoff the maximum of K minus S, and 0. The buyer pays a price up front, called the premium. An option with S above K is in the money; below K, it is out of the money.
[[slnc 400]]
The lesson of part c is a central idea of option theory. Since the payoff function is convex, it bends upward, E of the maximum of S minus K, and 0, is greater than or equal to the maximum of E of S minus K, and 0. This is Jensen's inequality. The option keeps the upside and cuts off the downside, so more uncertainty in S means a more valuable option. That is why option prices rise with volatility, the standard deviation of returns.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: underlying; strike; expiry; premium; in the money and out of the money; payoff diagram; volatility; convexity.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: so is 6 the price of this option?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: not exactly. The fair price today discounts the payoff, because money in a year is worth less than money today, and in option pricing theory the expectation is taken with special risk-neutral probabilities, not with the real-world probabilities of the exercise. But the structure, price equals the discounted expected payoff, is exactly this computation.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: why is the standard deviation of C, approximately 9.17, smaller than the standard deviation of S, 18, if options are risky?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: in euros the payoff varies less, but the option costs much less than the stock. Relative to the money invested, an option is far riskier: the payoff is 0 with probability 0.6, so the buyer loses the whole premium more often than not.

Exercise 9.37. A simple trading strategy, a finance example. Slides 41 and 42.

A day-trading strategy gains 300 euros with probability 0.55 and loses 200 euros with probability 0.45 on a given day. Let X be the daily profit and loss, P and L, in euros. Part a: find E of X, the variance of X and the standard deviation of X. Part b: the trader doubles the position size, so the daily P and L becomes 2 X. Find its expectation and standard deviation. Part c: instead of part b, the broker charges a fixed fee of 20 euros per day, so the daily P and L becomes X minus 20. Find its expectation and standard deviation.

[[slnc 500]]
Part a. The values of X: 300 with probability 0.55, and minus 200 with probability 0.45. A loss is a negative number. E of X equals 300 times 0.55, plus minus 200 times 0.45, which is 165 minus 90, which is 75. On average the strategy earns 75 euros a day.
[[slnc 400]]
For the variance, E of X squared first: E of X squared equals 300 squared times 0.55, plus minus 200, squared, times 0.45, which is 49,500 plus 18,000, which is 67,500. The variance of X equals E of X squared minus the square of E of X, which is 67,500 minus 75 squared, that is 67,500 minus 5,625, which is 61,875.
[[slnc 500]]
Next slide. 61,875 is in squared euros; back to euros: the standard deviation of X is the square root of 61,875, approximately 248.7 euros.
[[slnc 500]]
The typical deviation, about 249 euros, is much larger than the mean, 75 euros: on a single day the result is very uncertain.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board, the rules for a X plus b: E of a X plus b equals a times E of X, plus b, and the standard deviation of a X plus b equals the absolute value of a, times the standard deviation of X.

Part b.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: the position is doubled: what are a and b?
[[slnc 800]]
a is 2 and b is 0.
[[slnc 400]]
E of 2 X equals 2 times 75, which is 150, and the standard deviation of 2 X equals 2 times the standard deviation of X, approximately 497.5.
[[slnc 500]]
Leverage doubles the expected profit, but it doubles the risk too.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: the variance of 2 X equals 2 times the variance of X. The variance is multiplied by 4, it is 247,500; only the standard deviation is doubled.

Part c.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: a fixed fee of 20 euros: what are a and b?
[[slnc 800]]
a is 1 and b is minus 20.
[[slnc 400]]
E of X minus 20 equals 75 minus 20, which is 55, and the standard deviation of X minus 20 equals the standard deviation of X, approximately 248.7.
[[slnc 500]]
A constant shifts every outcome by the same amount: 280 and minus 220 instead of 300 and minus 200. The center moves, the spread does not change.

Background for you.
[[slnc 600]]
P and L, leverage and risk-adjusted return. P and L, profit and loss, is the money a trading account gains or loses over a period. Position size is how much is invested; doubling it doubles every gain and every loss. Leverage means taking a larger position with borrowed money. Volatility is the standard deviation of the P and L, or of returns: the standard measure of risk.
[[slnc 400]]
Risk-adjusted return. Traders compare strategies by the ratio of mean to standard deviation; this is the idea behind the Sharpe ratio. Here it is 75 over 248.7, approximately 0.30 per day. Leverage does not change it: 150 over 497.5 is approximately 0.30, because both numbers double. The fee lowers it: 55 over 248.7 is approximately 0.22. So leverage scales a strategy up or down, but it does not make it better; costs make it worse.
[[slnc 400]]
Key terms: P and L; position size; leverage; volatility; Sharpe ratio; transaction costs; margin call.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: if the expected profit doubles, why not use as much leverage as possible?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: because losses double too. A run of bad days can wipe out the account, and the broker demands more collateral, which is called a margin call, exactly when the trader is losing. Many trading firms failed this way.
[[slnc 400]]
A student may ask: is a 20-euro fee a big deal when the mean is 75?
[[slnc 400]]
Answer: it takes more than a quarter of the expected daily profit. For active strategies, transaction costs often decide whether the strategy is profitable at all.

Summary for Session 2. Slide 43.
[[slnc 600]]
A partition F 1 up to F n: P of E equals the sum, over i, of P of E given F i times P of F i, and the generalized Bayes formula gives P of F j given E: one path divided by all of them. Independence: P of E F equals P of E times P of F; for three events, all four product rules; pairwise independence is not enough. At least one in n independent trials: 1 minus the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n. The pmf, p of x, equals P of X equals x; the distribution function, F of x, equals P of X less than or equal to x, a step function. E of X is the sum of x times p of x; E of g of X is the sum of g of x times p of x, which is in general not g of E of X. The variance of X equals E of X squared minus the square of E of X; the variance of a X plus b equals a squared times the variance of X; and the standard deviation of X is the square root of the variance of X.
