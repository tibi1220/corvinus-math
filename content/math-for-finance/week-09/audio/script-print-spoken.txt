Almost every number in finance is uncertain: a default, next year's price, tomorrow's claims. Probability is the language for it.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: slide 3, the Session 1 title: click through to slide 4.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: the axioms, the rules, inclusion-exclusion, and a Venn diagram of two overlapping circles, E and F, inside the rectangle Omega. Its four regions are E and F complement, E and F in the middle, E complement and F, and the outside, E complement and F complement; they are mutually exclusive, and their probabilities add up to 1.
[[slnc 500]]
Three axioms: first, a probability is between 0 and 1; second, P of Omega equals 1; third, if E 1, E 2, and so on are mutually exclusive, the probability of their union is the sum of their probabilities. Everything else follows from these three: the complement rule, P of E complement equals 1 minus P of E; if E is a subset of F, then P of E is at most P of F; P of E and F complement equals P of E minus P of E and F; and the union rule, P of E union F equals P of E plus P of F minus P of E and F.
[[slnc 400]]
Why do these hold? Look at the picture. E and E complement do not overlap and together they fill Omega, so their probabilities add up to 1: that is the complement rule. E is made of two pieces that do not overlap, E and F, and E and F complement, so P of E is the sum of their probabilities: that is the third rule. And if we add P of E and P of F, the middle region E and F is counted twice, so we subtract it once: that is the union rule. For three events it is inclusion-exclusion, as for counts last week: P of E union F union G equals P of E plus P of F plus P of G, minus P of E and F, minus P of E and G, minus P of F and G, plus P of E, F and G.
[[slnc 500]]
The Venn diagram of two events has four regions that do not overlap; their probabilities add up to 1. That is the tool for the first exercise.

Equally likely outcomes. Slide 7.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: the classical definition. P of E equals the number of outcomes in E over the number of outcomes in Omega, that is, the number of favorable outcomes over the number of all outcomes.
[[slnc 500]]
If Omega is finite and all outcomes are equally likely, then P of E is favorable over all. Equally likely must come from the setup: a fair die, a well-shuffled deck, a choice at random. The counting methods of last week give the two numbers. And for at least one, use the complement: P of E equals 1 minus P of E complement.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: two fair dice, 36 equally likely outcomes. The sum is 7 in six of them, so the probability of a sum of 7 is 6 over 36, which is one sixth. At least one six: the complement, no six, has 5 times 5, that is 25 outcomes, so the answer is 1 minus 25 over 36, which is 11 over 36.
[[slnc 1200]]

Conditional probability. Slide 8.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: the definition, the multiplication rule, and a Venn diagram of E and F in which the circle F is shaded light and the overlap E and F dark. P of E given F is the share of the dark part within the circle F.
[[slnc 500]]
If P of F is greater than 0, the probability of E given F is P of E given F equals P of E and F over P of F. We know that F happened, so F becomes the new sample space, and only the part of E inside F counts.
[[slnc 400]]
In the picture: once we know F, everything outside the circle F is gone. The question is what share of the shaded circle is dark. That is why we divide by P of F: we rescale so that the new sample space, F, has probability 1.
[[slnc 500]]
Multiply through and you get the multiplication rule: P of E and F equals P of F times P of E given F. For three events, P of E 1, E 2 and E 3 equals P of E 1, times P of E 2 given E 1, times P of E 3 given E 1 and E 2. In words: the chance of both is the chance of the first, times the chance of the second once the first has happened. That is what we do when we multiply along the branches of a tree.
[[slnc 400]]
Finally, a probability given F is a probability itself, so all the rules of the last slide hold for it; for example P of E complement given F equals 1 minus P of E given F.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example: a fair die shows an even number. The chance it is a 6 is one sixth over one half, which is one third. Counting gives the same: the new sample space is 2, 4 and 6, and one of the three outcomes is a 6.

Total probability and Bayes' formula. Slide 11.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: clock check. This slide is planned for minute 27. If you start it after about minute 32, skip part c of Exercise 9.21.
[[slnc 500]]
So far we were given P of A and B, and we computed conditional probabilities from it. In practice it is often the other way round: we know conditional probabilities, and we want to turn them around. That is Bayes.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: Bayes' formula, then the law of total probability as the denominator, and a tree. From the root, two branches go to F and to F complement, with probabilities P of F and P of F complement; from each of them, two branches go to E and to E complement, with the conditional probabilities. The two leaves E are highlighted. Under the tree: multiply along a path; add the paths that end in E.
[[slnc 500]]
Bayes' formula: P of F given E equals P of E given F times P of F, over P of E. We usually know P of E given F, but we need P of F given E: Bayes' formula turns the condition around. Where does it come from? The numerator is P of E and F by the multiplication rule, so this is just the definition, P of F given E equals P of E and F over P of E, with the numerator written in a form we can compute.
[[slnc 500]]
The denominator, P of E, comes from the law of total probability. E happens either together with F or together with F complement, never both, so P of E equals P of E given F times P of F, plus P of E given F complement times P of F complement.
[[slnc 500]]
On the tree: multiply along a path; add the paths that end in E. That is the denominator. Bayes is then one path divided by all the paths ending in E. Written out in one line: P of F given E equals P of E given F times P of F, divided by the sum of P of E given F times P of F and P of E given F complement times P of F complement.
[[slnc 400]]
The names: P of F is the prior, what we believe before we see E; P of F given E is the posterior, what we believe after. Bayes is the rule for updating beliefs with evidence.
[[slnc 1200]]

Why Bayes' formula works: a picture. Slide 12.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: this frame is the visual proof. Take your time with it, it pays off in the next three exercises. Point at the shapes at each step. The last row comes with a click. If you are far behind, show only the last row and the two cancellations.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: at the top, a gray box with rounded corners, the sample space Omega. Inside it, a blue diamond, F, and a light blue circle, E. They overlap, and their overlap, F E, is red. Next to it, two lines: the probability of an event is its share of Omega, the gray box; a conditional probability is the share inside the shape we know happened. Below, a row of four little fractions made of shapes. P of F is a small diamond over a small gray box. P of E is a small circle over a small gray box. P of F given E is the small red piece over a small circle. P of E given F is the small red piece over a small diamond. After a click, the last row: P of F given E equals P of E given F times P of F, over P of E, which equals red piece over diamond, times diamond over box, all over circle over box. The diamond below and the diamond above are crossed out in red, and so are the two boxes. What is left is the red piece over the circle, and that is P of F given E, by the definition.
[[slnc 500]]
Here is why Bayes' formula is true, in one picture. No numbers, only shapes.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 1: the picture. Point at the top. The gray box is the whole sample space Omega. The blue diamond is the hypothesis F, say, the borrower defaults. The light blue circle is the observation E, the model flags the borrower. Where they overlap, both happen: the red piece is F E.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 2: probability is a share. Point at the second row. P of F is the share of the box that the diamond covers. Read the little fraction as diamond over box. In the same way, P of E is circle over box.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 3: conditional probability is a share inside what we know. Suppose we know that E happened. Then the outcome is somewhere in the circle, and the rest of the box no longer counts: the circle is our new box. Inside the circle, F happens exactly in the red piece. So P of F given E is red over circle. The other way around, if we know F happened, the diamond is the new box, and P of E given F is red over diamond. Point at the last two fractions of the row. Same red piece, different denominators: that is why P of E given F and P of F given E are different numbers.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: look at the picture. Which is bigger here, P of F given E, or P of E given F?
[[slnc 800]]
The red piece is the same; the circle is bigger than the diamond, so red over circle is smaller: in this picture, P of F given E is less than P of E given F. With a bigger diamond it would be the other way.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 4: plug the shapes into the formula. Click: the last row. Take the right-hand side of Bayes' formula and replace each probability by its fraction of shapes. The numerator, P of E given F times P of F, is red over diamond, times diamond over box. Point at the two diamonds: one is below, one is above, so they cancel. What is left is red over box, that is P of F E, the multiplication rule. The denominator, P of E, is circle over box.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 5: the boxes cancel. Point at the two crossed-out boxes: we divide red over box by circle over box, and the box drops out.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 6: read what is left. Red over circle. By step 3, that is exactly P of F given E, the share of the red piece in the circle. So the right-hand side equals P of F given E, and that is Bayes' formula.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board, if you want to write it: F E over F, times F over Omega, all over E over Omega, equals F E over E, the shapes standing for their sizes.
[[slnc 500]]
In one sentence: the red piece can be measured from two sides. From the diamond's side, P of F E equals P of F times P of E given F. From the circle's side, P of F E equals P of E times P of F given E. Same piece, so the two are equal; divide by P of E.
[[slnc 400]]
Keep this picture in mind for the next exercise. When the diamond is tiny, even a large share of it inside the circle is a small piece of the circle: P of E given F can be large while P of F given E stays small.

[[slnc 400]]

[[slnc 800]]
The borrowers who are not flagged.
[[slnc 400]]

Odds. Slide 15.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: two blocks. The odds of H equal P of H over P of H complement, which is P of H over 1 minus P of H, and back, P of H equals the odds over 1 plus the odds. Then Bayes' formula in odds form: the new odds, P of H given E over P of H complement given E, equal the old odds, P of H over P of H complement, times the likelihood ratio, P of E given H over P of E given H complement. Under it: evidence that is more likely if H is true raises the odds of H.
[[slnc 500]]
The odds of H are P of H over P of H complement, and back, P of H equals the odds over 1 plus the odds. Probability two thirds means odds 2, 2 to 1 in favor; 0.2 means odds one quarter, 4 to 1 against. Odds are how bookmakers and traders often quote chances.
[[slnc 500]]
Bayes' formula in odds form: the new odds equal the old odds times the likelihood ratio. Why is it true? Write Bayes' formula for H and for H complement, with the same evidence E: P of H given E equals P of E given H times P of H, over P of E, and P of H complement given E equals P of E given H complement times P of H complement, over P of E. Divide the first by the second: the awkward denominator P of E cancels. That is the whole proof, and it is why the odds form is so quick: no total probability needed.
[[slnc 500]]
Let us redo the early-warning model in one line. Old odds of default: 0.02 over 0.98, which is 1 over 49. A flag is 0.9 over 0.05, that is 18 times as likely for a defaulter. New odds: 18 over 49, so P of D given A equals 18 over 18 plus 49, which is 18 over 67, approximately 0.269. The same answer, with one multiplication.

The generalized Bayes formula. Slide 16.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: clock check. This slide is planned for minute 47. If you start it after about minute 52, also skip part d of Exercise 9.27.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: the definition of a partition; the generalized Bayes formula, P of F j given E equals P of E given F j times P of F j, over P of E; and total probability as the denominator, P of E equals the sum, for i from 1 to n, of P of E given F i times P of F i. On the right, a tree: from the root, three branches to F 1, F 2 and F 3, labeled P of F 1, P of F 2 and P of F 3, and from each F i one branch to E, labeled P of E given F i. Under it: one path to E for each F i.
[[slnc 500]]
Now more than two hypotheses. F 1 to F n is a partition of Omega: mutually exclusive events, and exactly one of them occurs. Think of rating classes, or economic scenarios: boom, normal, recession.
[[slnc 500]]
Bayes' formula is the same as before, and the denominator again comes from total probability, now with one term for each F i.
[[slnc 500]]
Point at the tree. The root now has n branches, one for each F i, and each is followed by a branch to E. Multiply along a path to get P of F i times P of E given F i. Add all the paths to get P of E. Bayes is one path divided by all of them.
[[slnc 400]]
The posteriors, P of F j given E, add up to 1: a free check.

Independent events. Slide 20.
[[slnc 600]]
In every exercise so far, information changed the probability. Sometimes it does not. That is independence.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: two blocks and two bullet points. Two events are independent if P of E and F equals P of E times P of F. For three events, all four product rules: the three pairs, P of E and F, P of E and G, and P of F and G, each equal to the product, and P of E, F and G equal to P of E times P of F times P of G.
[[slnc 500]]
E and F are independent if P of E and F equals P of E times P of F. If P of F is greater than 0, this means P of E given F equals P of E: knowing F does not change the chances of E. To see it, divide the product rule by P of F: P of E given F equals P of E and F over P of F, which is P of E. Then E and F complement are independent too: if knowing F tells you nothing, knowing not F tells you nothing either.
[[slnc 500]]
For three events all four product rules must hold: the three pairs, and also P of E, F and G equals P of E times P of F times P of G.
[[slnc 500]]
Two warnings. Mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent: if one occurs, the other cannot, so knowing one changes the other a lot. And for n independent trials with success probability p, P of at least one success equals 1 minus the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n, because the complement, all n trials fail, has probability 1 minus p, to the power n, by the product rule.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny examples: two fair dice; first is a 6 and second is a 6 are independent, and the probability of both is one sixth times one sixth, which is 1 over 36. And independence can hold without any physical separation: one die, E is even, F is 1 or 2. Then E and F is just the outcome 2, and its probability, one sixth, equals one half times one third: independent. Check the product, do not trust intuition.

Discrete random variables. Slide 27.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: the definitions of the probability mass function and the distribution function, and the graph of F for an example with p of 1 equal to one quarter, p of 2 equal to one half, and p of 3 and p of 4 both equal to one eighth. The graph is a staircase: 0 up to 1, then steps up to one quarter at 1, three quarters at 2, seven eighths at 3, and 1 at 4, with a filled dot at the top of each jump and an open dot at the bottom.
[[slnc 500]]
A random variable X assigns a number to every outcome. It is discrete if it takes finitely or countably many values. Its probability mass function, the pmf, is p of x equals P of X equals x; p of x is greater than or equal to 0, and the sum of p of x over all x is 1. The pmf is the complete list: which values, with which probabilities.
[[slnc 500]]
The distribution function is F of x equals P of X less than or equal to x, which is the sum of p of y over all y less than or equal to x. For a discrete X it is a step function: it jumps by p of x at every value x, is constant in between, and rises from 0 to 1.
[[slnc 400]]
Read the graph from left to right. Below 1, X cannot be that small, so F is 0. At 1 it jumps by p of 1, one quarter; at 2 by one half, to three quarters; at 3 by one eighth, to seven eighths; at 4 by one eighth, to 1, and stays there. The filled dots show that at a jump F takes the upper value: F of 2, P of X less than or equal to 2, includes the value 2 itself.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: P of a less than X less than or equal to b equals F of b minus F of a, and P of X greater than a equals 1 minus F of a.
[[slnc 400]]
Example from the graph: P of 1 less than X less than or equal to 3 equals F of 3 minus F of 1, which is seven eighths minus one quarter, which is five eighths; that is p of 2 plus p of 3, one half plus one eighth. And P of X greater than 2 equals 1 minus F of 2, which is one quarter.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you, a common mistake: P of X less than 2 equals F of 2. For a discrete X, less than and less than or equal to differ: P of X less than 2 is F of 1, one quarter, while F of 2 is three quarters.

Expected value, variance, standard deviation. Slide 28.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: if you start this slide after about minute 12, give only the standard deviation of C, approximately 9.17, in part b of Exercise 9.36.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: two blocks with the formulas. The expected value of X is the sum of x times p of x; the expected value of g of X is the sum of g of x times p of x; and the expected value of a X plus b equals a times the expected value of X, plus b. With mu equal to the expected value of X, the variance of X is the expected value of the square of X minus mu, which equals the expected value of X squared minus the square of the expected value of X; the standard deviation is the square root of the variance. And the variance of a X plus b equals a squared times the variance of X, and the standard deviation of a X plus b equals the absolute value of a times the standard deviation of X.
[[slnc 500]]
The expected value of X is the sum of x times p of x: the average of the values, weighted by their probabilities. If you repeat the experiment many times, the average of the observed values settles near the expected value. For a function, the expected value of g of X is the sum of g of x times p of x: apply g to each value, keep the probabilities. And the expected value of a X plus b equals a times the expected value of X, plus b: change of units passes straight through.
[[slnc 500]]
With mu equal to the expected value of X, the variance is the expected value of the square of X minus mu: the average squared distance from the mean. To compute it, use the shortcut: the expected value of X squared minus the square of the expected value of X, and take the square root for the standard deviation. The standard deviation is back in the units of X, so it is the number we interpret: a typical distance from the mean. And the variance of a X plus b equals a squared times the variance of X: adding a constant does not change the spread, and multiplying by a multiplies the standard deviation by the absolute value of a.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example with the pmf of the last slide: the expected value of X is 1 times one quarter, plus 2 times one half, plus 3 times one eighth, plus 4 times one eighth, which is 2.125. The expected value of X squared is 1 times one quarter, plus 4 times one half, plus 9 times one eighth, plus 16 times one eighth, which is 5.375. So the variance of X is 5.375 minus 2.125 squared, approximately 0.859, and the standard deviation of X is approximately 0.927.
[[slnc 500]]
In finance: the expected value of X is the expected return or payoff, and the standard deviation of X measures the risk.

Bernoulli distribution. Slide 31.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: clock check. This slide is planned for minute 20. If you start it after about minute 25, skip part e of Exercise 9.42.
[[slnc 500]]
Now the named distributions. Each one comes with a story; if the story fits the problem, the formulas are ready to use. The simplest story first.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: the Bernoulli distribution with parameter p. One trial with two outcomes: success with probability p, failure with probability 1 minus p; X equals 1 on success and X equals 0 on failure. So P of X equals 1 equals p, and P of X equals 0 equals 1 minus p. The expected value of X is 1 times p, plus 0 times 1 minus p, which is p; the variance of X is the expected value of X squared minus p squared, which is p minus p squared, that is, p times the quantity 1 minus p. Two bullet points: examples, a loan defaults or not, a call ends in a sale or not, a coin shows heads or not; and X is an indicator: it counts 1 if the event happens, so the expected value of X is the probability of the event.
[[slnc 500]]
A Bernoulli variable is a yes-or-no question turned into a number: 1 for yes, 0 for no. Its mean is 1 times p, plus 0 times 1 minus p, which is p. For the variance: X is 0 or 1, so X squared equals X, and the expected value of X squared is p; then the variance of X is p minus p squared, which is p times the quantity 1 minus p.
[[slnc 500]]
The key sentence: the expected value of an indicator is the probability of its event. Counting with indicators is how we get the binomial mean on the next slide.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: a loan defaults with probability 0.05; X equals 1 if it defaults. The expected value of X is 0.05, and the variance of X is 0.05 times 0.95, which is 0.0475.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: for which p is the variance largest?
[[slnc 800]]
For p equals one half: then p times 1 minus p is one quarter. At p equals 0 or p equals 1 there is no uncertainty, and the variance is 0.

Binomial distribution. Slide 32.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: the binomial distribution with parameters n and p. X is the number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials, each a success with the same probability p. P of X equals i equals n choose i, times p to the power i, times the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n minus i, for i equal to 0, 1, and so on up to n; the expected value is n p, and the variance is n p times the quantity 1 minus p. Three bullet points: n choose i says which i of the n trials are the successes, and p to the power i, times 1 minus p to the power n minus i, is the probability of one such order; X is the sum of n Bernoulli variables, so its mean and variance are n times those of one trial; and the binomial with n equals 1 is the Bernoulli distribution.
[[slnc 500]]
The binomial story: n independent trials, each a success with the same probability p; X counts the successes. Why this formula? One order with i successes and n minus i failures has probability p to the power i, times the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n minus i: by independence we multiply. There are n choose i such orders, one for each choice of the i successful trials, and they are mutually exclusive, so we add.
[[slnc 500]]
Why n p and n p times 1 minus p? X is the sum of X 1 up to X n, where X k is the Bernoulli indicator of a success on trial k. Expected values add, so the expected value of X is n p; for independent trials variances add too, so the variance of X is n p times the quantity 1 minus p. And with n equals 1 we are back at the Bernoulli distribution.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: three fair coin flips, X is the number of heads. P of X equals 2 equals 3 choose 2, times 0.5 squared, times 0.5, which is 3 over 8: the three orders H H T, H T H and T H H, each with probability one eighth. And the expected value of X is 3 times 0.5, which is 1.5.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: what are the three conditions to check before using the binomial?
[[slnc 800]]
A fixed number of trials; independent; the same p every time.

Poisson distribution. Slide 34.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: the Poisson distribution with parameter lambda greater than 0: P of X equals i equals e to the minus lambda, times lambda to the power i, over i factorial, for i equal to 0, 1, 2, and so on; the expected value and the variance are both lambda. Then two bullet points on rare events, and the Poisson approximation of the binomial.
[[slnc 500]]
The Poisson story: the number of rare events in a fixed period: claims a day, defaults a year, errors on a page. With parameter lambda greater than 0 the pmf is the formula above, and the expected value and the variance of X are both lambda: lambda is the average number of events in the period.
[[slnc 500]]
If n is large and p is small, the binomial with parameters n and p is close to the Poisson with lambda equal to n p. Many independent chances, each small, counted together: that is where the Poisson comes from.
[[slnc 400]]
Example: an insurer has 1000 policies, each with a claim on a given day with probability 0.003. The number of claims is binomial with parameters 1000 and 0.003, close to the Poisson with lambda equal to 3. Geometric distribution. Slide 36.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: clock check. This slide is planned for minute 45. If you start it after about minute 50, also skip part d of Exercise 9.44.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: the geometric distribution with parameter p: P of X equals n equals the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n minus 1, times p, for n equal to 1, 2, and so on; the expected value is 1 over p and the variance is 1 minus p, over p squared. Two bullet points: P of X greater than n equals the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n; and the memoryless property, P of X greater than n plus k, given X greater than n, equals P of X greater than k.
[[slnc 500]]
The geometric story: independent trials with success probability p; X is the number of the trial on which the first success happens. Then P of X equals n equals the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n minus 1, times p, with mean 1 over p and variance 1 minus p, over p squared. Why this formula? The first success on trial n means: n minus 1 failures, then one success, and by independence we multiply.
[[slnc 400]]
P of X greater than n equals the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n: the first n trials are all failures. And it is memoryless: P of X greater than n plus k, given X greater than n, equals P of X greater than k. Past failures do not make a success due.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: roll a die until the first six, p equals one sixth. On average it takes 6 rolls. No six in the first six rolls: five sixths to the power 6, approximately 0.335, so a third of the time it takes longer than the average.

Hypergeometric distribution. Slide 39.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: the hypergeometric distribution: P of X equals i equals m choose i, times capital N minus m choose n minus i, over capital N choose n, and the expected value of X is n times m over capital N. Two bullet points: without replacement the picks are not independent, so X is not binomial; and if capital N is large compared with n, it is close to the binomial with parameters n and m over capital N.
[[slnc 500]]
The hypergeometric story: from capital N items, m of which are special, we pick n at random without replacement; X counts the special ones picked. The formula is favorable over all, with last week's counting: choose i of the m special items and n minus i of the capital N minus m others, out of all ways to choose n of capital N.
[[slnc 400]]
Without replacement the picks are not independent, so X is not binomial: each special item drawn makes the next one less likely. If capital N is large compared with n, removing a few items hardly matters, and it is close to the binomial with parameters n and m over capital N.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: an urn with 5 balls, 2 of them red; pick 2. Exactly one red: 2 choose 1 times 3 choose 1, over 5 choose 2, which is 6 over 10, which is 0.6.

Summary for Session 2. Slide 41.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: a table of the five distributions. Bernoulli with parameter p: P of X equals 1 is p; mean p; variance p times 1 minus p. Binomial with parameters n and p: P of X equals i is n choose i, times p to the power i, times 1 minus p to the power n minus i; mean n p; variance n p times 1 minus p. Poisson with parameter lambda: e to the minus lambda, times lambda to the power i, over i factorial; mean and variance lambda. Geometric with parameter p: 1 minus p to the power i minus 1, times p; mean 1 over p; variance 1 minus p, over p squared. Hypergeometric: m choose i, times capital N minus m choose n minus i, over capital N choose n; mean n m over capital N. Under the table, two bullet points and the self-study exercises.
[[slnc 500]]
Session 2 in a few lines: the expected value of X is the sum of x times p of x; the variance of X is the expected value of X squared minus the square of the expected value of X; and the standard deviation of X is the square root of the variance of X. The table has the five distributions; the key is the story. One trial, success or not: Bernoulli. A fixed number of independent trials: binomial, a sum of Bernoulli variables. Rare events in a period: Poisson. Waiting for the first success: geometric. Sampling without replacement: hypergeometric. And for at least one or at least two, use the complement.
