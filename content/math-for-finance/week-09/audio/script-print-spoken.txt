Basic principle of counting. Slide 4.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: the counting tree. From the root, m branches; each of them splits into n branches, and the leaves are the pairs 1 1, 1 2, up to m n. A brace marks the m times n leaves.
[[slnc 500]]
The basic principle of counting: if something can be done in m ways, and then, for each of those, a second thing in n ways, then the two together can be done in m times n ways. With more stages we keep multiplying: n 1 times n 2, and so on, up to n r.
[[slnc 500]]
On the board: m ways, then n ways, gives m times n ways.
[[slnc 500]]
Think of the tree on the slide: the first stage gives m branches, each branch splits into n branches, so there are m times n leaves. Every leaf is one outcome, a pair: first choice, second choice. Three shirts and four pairs of trousers: 3 times 4, that is 12 outfits. Everything else we do in counting comes from this one principle.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: a bank code has two letters, out of 26, followed by three digits. How many codes are there?
[[slnc 800]]
26 times 26 times 10 times 10 times 10, that is 676 thousand: five stages, multiply.
[[slnc 500]]
One remark: the number of ways in the second stage must not depend on what we chose in the first stage. The choices themselves may depend on it, the second draw cannot repeat the first, but their number must be the same. That is how we get n times n minus 1 on the next slide.
[[slnc 1200]]

Two questions to ask every time. Slide 5.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: the table. Ordered, no repetition: n factorial over n minus k factorial; the example is 5 times 4, that is 20: a b, b a, a c, c a, up to d e, e d. Unordered, no repetition: n choose k; the example is 5 choose 2, that is 10: a b, a c, a d, a e, b c, b d, b e, c d, c e, d e. Ordered, with repetition: n to the power k; the example is 5 squared, that is 25: a a, a b, b a, b b, up to e e. Unordered, with repetition: n plus k minus 1, choose k; the example is 6 choose 2, that is 15: a a, a b, up to e e.
[[slnc 500]]
Before every counting exercise, ask yourself two questions. First: does the order matter? Second: can the same element be used more than once? The two answers put you in one of the four cells of the table.
[[slnc 500]]
Let us derive the cells quickly; each one is the basic principle.
[[slnc 500]]
Ordered, no repetition. The first place can be filled in n ways, the second in n minus 1 ways, one element is used up, and so on; after k places we stop: n times n minus 1, down to n minus k plus 1, which is n factorial over n minus k factorial. With k equal to n this is n factorial, the number of orderings of n objects.
[[slnc 500]]
Unordered, no repetition. If only the set of chosen elements matters, look at the ordered choices: every set of k elements appears in k factorial orders, so it was counted k factorial times. Divide: n choose k equals n factorial over k factorial times n minus k factorial.
[[slnc 500]]
Ordered, with repetition. Every one of the k places can be any of the n elements: n to the power k.
[[slnc 500]]
Unordered, with repetition. This one is n plus k minus 1, choose k; we see why on slide 8, with stars and bars.

[[slnc 500]]
Click. Take Omega equals a, b, c, d, e, and choose k equals 2. Ordered, no repetition: 5 times 4, that is 20 pairs, a b, b a, and so on up to e d. Unordered: a b and b a are the same set, so we get half of it, 5 choose 2, that is 10. With repetition, ordered: 5 squared, that is 25; now a a, b b and so on are allowed.

Multinomial coefficient. Slide 6.
[[slnc 600]]
Now a question that looks different: in how many ways can we divide n distinct objects into r labeled groups of given sizes n 1 up to n r?
[[slnc 500]]
Fill the groups one after the other. The first group can be chosen in n choose n 1 ways; the second from the remaining n minus n 1 objects in n minus n 1 choose n 2 ways; and so on, until the last group takes what is left, in one way. Multiply and cancel the factorials: we get the multinomial coefficient, n factorial over n 1 factorial, times n 2 factorial, and so on, up to n r factorial.
[[slnc 500]]
This is the same number as for arranging letters when some of them are identical, as in MISSISSIPPI, Exercise 8.8. And that is no coincidence: write under every object the name of its group. You get a word with n 1 letters 1, n 2 letters 2, and so on. A division into groups is a word with repeated letters.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example: four people into two labeled rooms, two in each: 4 factorial over 2 factorial times 2 factorial, that is 6. The words are 1 1 2 2, 1 2 1 2, 1 2 2 1, 2 1 1 2, 2 1 2 1, and 2 2 1 1.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: with r equals 2 groups, what is the multinomial coefficient n over k and n minus k?
[[slnc 800]]
It is n choose k: choosing the first group fixes the second.

Binomial and multinomial theorems. Slide 9.
[[slnc 600]]
Why is the coefficient of a to the k times b to the n minus k equal to n choose k? When you multiply out a plus b, times a plus b, and so on, you choose a or b from every factor. To get a to the k times b to the n minus k you must choose a from exactly k factors, and there are n choose k ways to choose which ones. That is why the numbers n choose k are called binomial coefficients.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: the slide mentions Pascal's triangle before it appears: say, which we see on the next slide.
[[slnc 500]]
The coefficients of a plus b to the n form row n of Pascal's triangle, which we see on the next slide.
[[slnc 500]]
With more than two terms it is the same idea. To get x 1 to the n 1, and so on, up to x r to the n r, we take x 1 from n 1 of the factors, x 2 from n 2 of them, and so on. That is a division of the n factors into labeled groups of given sizes: the multinomial coefficient of slide 6.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: what is the first formula for n equals 2?
[[slnc 800]]
a squared plus 2 a b plus b squared: the coefficients 1, 2, 1.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: what is the coefficient of x y z in x plus y plus z, cubed?
[[slnc 800]]
The multinomial coefficient 3 over 1, 1, 1, that is 3 factorial over 1 times 1 times 1, which is 6: each of the three factors gives a different letter, in 3 factorial orders.
[[slnc 500]]
One trick worth remembering: set a and b equal to 1 and you get that the sum of n choose k over all k is 2 to the n. A set with n elements has 2 to the n subsets, since every element is in or out.

Pascal's triangle. Slide 10.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: Pascal's triangle, rows 0 to 6: 1; 1 1; 1 2 1; 1 3 3 1; 1 4 6 4 1; 1 5 10 10 5 1; 1 6 15 20 15 6 1. Two gold circles mark the 4 and the 6 in row 4, with arrows to the 10 below them in row 5.
[[slnc 500]]
Write the numbers n choose k in rows: row n contains n choose 0, n choose 1, up to n choose n. This is Pascal's triangle.
[[slnc 500]]
On the board: n choose k plus n choose k plus 1 equals n plus 1 choose k plus 1; for example 4 choose 1 plus 4 choose 2, that is 4 plus 6, equals 10, which is 5 choose 2.
[[slnc 500]]
This rule, Pascal's rule, builds the triangle: each entry is the sum of the two entries above it. Why is it true? Choose k plus 1 people out of n plus 1, and fix one person, say Anna. Either Anna is chosen, and we choose the other k from the remaining n: n choose k ways; or she is not, and we choose all k plus 1 from the remaining n: n choose k plus 1 ways. That is the proof in Exercise 8.21.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: who can tell me the next row, n equals 7?
[[slnc 800]]
1, 7, 21, 35, 35, 21, 7, 1: each entry the sum of the two above it.

Second, the coefficients of a plus b to the n, the binomial theorem of the last slide; for example, row 5 gives a plus b to the fifth equals a to the fifth, plus 5 a to the fourth b, plus 10 a cubed b squared, plus 10 a squared b cubed, plus 5 a b to the fourth, plus b to the fifth. Sample space and events. Slide 11.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: six small Venn diagrams of two circles, E and F, in the rectangle Omega, with the event shaded. Top row: E, F, and E minus F, which is E F complement. Bottom row: E union F, E F, and E complement.
[[slnc 500]]
Now we switch to the language of probability. The sample space Omega is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. An event is a subset E of Omega. We say that E occurs if the outcome of the experiment is an element of E.
[[slnc 500]]
Because events are sets, we can combine them. E union F: E or F occurs, at least one of them. E intersect F, often written E F: both occur. E complement: E does not occur. E a subset of F: whenever E occurs, F occurs too; E implies F. And E and F are mutually exclusive if E F is empty: they cannot occur together.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example: roll a die, Omega is 1 to 6, E is even, that is 2, 4, 6, and F is at least 5, that is 5 and 6. Then E union F is 2, 4, 5, 6; E F is just 6; and E complement is 1, 3, 5.
[[slnc 500]]
A Venn diagram is a picture of this: the rectangle is Omega, the circles are the events, and the shaded region is the event we are talking about. Top row: E, F, and E minus F, E but not F: the left circle without the lens. In event notation this is E F complement. Bottom row: E union F shades both circles, E F only the lens, E complement everything outside the left circle.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: which picture shows F occurs but E does not?
[[slnc 800]]
None of the six: it is E complement F, F minus E, the right circle without the lens.

De Morgan's laws. Slides 12 and 13.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: a table of Venn diagrams. First row: E complement, shaded teal, intersect F complement, shaded gold, equals the region outside both circles, which is the complement of E union F. Second row: E complement union F complement equals everything but the lens, which is the complement of E intersect F.
[[slnc 500]]
On the board: the complement of E union F equals E complement intersect F complement; the complement of E intersect F equals E complement union F complement.
[[slnc 500]]
In words: not, E or F, is the same as neither E nor F. And not both E and F is the same as at least one of them fails.
[[slnc 500]]
Look at the pictures. Shade E complement, everything outside E, in one color, and F complement in another. The region shaded twice is the intersection, E complement intersect F complement: the part outside both circles, which is exactly the complement of E union F. The region shaded at least once is the union, E complement union F complement: everything except the lens in the middle, and the lens is E intersect F. So the union is the complement of E intersect F.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: give me an everyday example of the second law.
[[slnc 800]]
It is not true that I passed both exams equals I failed at least one of them.
[[slnc 500]]
Clock check: slide 13, planned minute 37. If you are more than about 5 minutes late, in Exercise 8.34, slide 16, give parts c and d as answers only: 3 and 4 outcomes, one sentence each; about 3 minutes saved.
[[slnc 500]]
The same laws hold for any number of events: the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and the complement of an intersection is the union of the complements. Not at least one of them is none of them; not all of them is at least one fails.
[[slnc 500]]
In a minute we use exactly this: P of A complement B complement equals 1 minus P of A union B. Neither A nor B is the complement of at least one.

Exercise 8.32. Events of a stock path. Slide 15.
[[slnc 600]]
Finance context: a stock moves Up or Down on each of 3 days, a three-step binomial tree. Questions like did the stock end above its starting price, or did it ever fall below it, are events; some options, for example barrier options, pay depending on such path properties.
[[slnc 800]]
A stock moves Up or Down on each of 3 days: Omega is U U U, U U D, U D U, U D D, D U U, D U D, D D U, D D D. Let A be the stock ends above the start, B be up on day 1, and C be never below the start. List A, B and C, and find A B, A complement B, and A B complement. Which inclusion holds between A and C?
[[slnc 800]]
Try it in pairs, three minutes. A hint: track the position, the number of ups minus the number of downs, after each day.
[[slnc 500]]
Note for you: show the solution, and go through it part by part; ask first.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: which paths end above the start?
[[slnc 800]]
Those with at least 2 U's.
[[slnc 500]]
A: at least 2 U's, so the net position is plus 1 or plus 3: A is U U U, U U D, U D U, D U U. B: the first letter is U: B is U U U, U U D, U D U, U D D. C: the positions must stay at least 0. U D D goes plus 1, 0, minus 1, so it drops below; any path starting with D is at minus 1 after day 1. Hence C is U U U, U U D, U D U.
[[slnc 500]]

[[slnc 800]]
After 3 steps the position is odd, so never below forces it to end at plus 1 or more: C is a subset of A.

[[slnc 600]]

[[slnc 800]]

Adding the three sets counts the pairwise overlaps twice, so we subtract them; but then the center has been added three times and subtracted three times, so we add it back once. For two sets it is simply the size of E plus the size of F minus the size of E F.

From counting to probability. Slide 18.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: a Venn diagram of two overlapping circles, E and F, inside the rectangle Omega. Its four regions are E F complement, E F in the middle, E complement F, and the outside, E complement F complement; they are mutually exclusive, and their probabilities add up to 1.
[[slnc 500]]
A probability P gives every event a number, and it behaves like the share of the clients in the last exercise. Three axioms: first, a probability is between 0 and 1; second, P of Omega equals 1; third, if E 1, E 2, and so on are mutually exclusive, the probability of their union is the sum of their probabilities. Everything else follows from these three: the complement rule, P of E complement equals 1 minus P of E; if E is a subset of F, then P of E is at most P of F; P of E F complement equals P of E minus P of E F; and the union rule, P of E union F equals P of E plus P of F minus P of E F.
[[slnc 500]]
Why do these hold? Look at the picture. E and E complement do not overlap and together they fill Omega, so their probabilities add up to 1: that is the complement rule. E is made of two pieces that do not overlap, E F and E F complement, so P of E is the sum of their probabilities: that is the third rule. If E is a subset of F, then F is E plus a piece that does not overlap it, with a probability of at least 0: that is the second rule. And if we add P of E and P of F, the middle region E F is counted twice, so we subtract it once: that is the union rule. For three events it is inclusion-exclusion, exactly as for the counts of the bank clients: P of E union F union G equals P of E plus P of F plus P of G, minus P of E F, minus P of E G, minus P of F G, plus P of E F G.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: in the bank, what is the probability that a random client has a current account but no credit card?
[[slnc 800]]
P of C K complement equals P of C minus P of C K, 0.82 minus 0.30, which is 0.52: the third rule.
[[slnc 500]]
The Venn diagram of two events has four regions that do not overlap; their probabilities add up to 1. That is the tool for the next exercise.

Session 1 title, equally likely outcomes. Slides 21 and 22.
[[slnc 600]]
If Omega is finite and all outcomes are equally likely, then P of E is the number of outcomes in E over the number of outcomes in Omega: favorable over all.
[[slnc 400]]
Why is that true? If there are N outcomes and all have the same probability, the axioms say the N probabilities add up to 1, so each one is 1 over N. An event with k outcomes then has probability k over N.
[[slnc 400]]
Equally likely must come from the setup: a fair die, a well-shuffled deck, a choice at random. It is an assumption, not a fact we can prove. The counting methods of last week give the two numbers. And for at least one, use the complement: P of E equals 1 minus P of E complement.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: two fair dice, 6 times 6, that is 36 equally likely ordered outcomes. The sum is 7 in six of them, 1 and 6, 2 and 5, and so on up to 6 and 1, so the probability of a sum of 7 is 6 over 36, which is one sixth. At least one six: the complement, no six, has 5 times 5, that is 25 outcomes, so the answer is 1 minus 25 over 36, which is 11 over 36.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: why do we count ordered pairs for two dice, and not just the 21 unordered results?
[[slnc 800]]
The ordered pairs are equally likely; the unordered ones are not: a 1 and a 2 can happen in two ways, two 1s in only one.

Conditional probability. Slide 23.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: a Venn diagram of E and F in which the circle F is shaded light and the overlap E F dark. P of E given F is the share of the dark part within the circle F.
[[slnc 500]]
If P of F is greater than 0, the probability of E given F is P of E given F equals P of E and F over P of F. We know that F happened, so F becomes the new sample space, and only the part of E inside F counts.
[[slnc 400]]
In the picture: once we know F, everything outside the circle F is gone. The question is what share of the shaded circle is dark. That is why we divide by P of F: we rescale so that the new sample space, F, has probability 1.
[[slnc 500]]
Multiply through and you get the multiplication rule: P of E and F equals P of F times P of E given F. For three events, P of E 1, E 2 and E 3 equals P of E 1, times P of E 2 given E 1, times P of E 3 given E 1 and E 2. In words: the chance of both is the chance of the first, times the chance of the second once the first has happened. That is what we do when we multiply along the branches of a tree.
[[slnc 400]]
Finally, a probability given F is a probability itself, so all the rules of probability hold for it; for example P of E complement given F equals 1 minus P of E given F.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example: a fair die shows an even number. The chance it is a 6 is P of 6 over P of even, one sixth over one half, which is one third. Counting gives the same: the new sample space is 2, 4 and 6, and one of the three outcomes is a 6.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: and the chance it is a 5, given that it is even?
[[slnc 800]]
0: the 5 is outside the new sample space.

Why do we need Bayes' formula? Slide 26.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: three steps on this slide, two clicks: the motivation, then, click, the example Skill or luck, then, click, what we know and what we want. Collect gut feelings before you move on; the answer comes on slide 28.
[[slnc 500]]
So far we were given the probability of the overlap, and we computed conditional probabilities from it. In practice the data usually come in the wrong direction.
[[slnc 400]]
Think of how we learn things. From history, or from testing a model, we know how the evidence behaves under each hypothesis: how often a defaulting borrower gets flagged by a model, how often a skilled investor beats the market. That is P of E given F and P of E given F complement: evidence given hypothesis.
[[slnc 400]]
But a decision needs the reverse: this borrower is flagged; how likely is it that they default? This manager has beaten the market; how likely is it that they are skilled? That is P of F given E: hypothesis given evidence.
[[slnc 400]]
These two are different numbers. P of E given F is not equal to P of F given E in general. Bayes' formula turns the condition around: it updates what we believed before the evidence, P of F, into what we should believe after it, P of F given E.
[[slnc 500]]
Click. An example from asset management. Only 10 percent of fund managers are really skilled. A skilled manager beats the market in a given year with probability 0.7; an unskilled one with probability 0.5, a coin flip. Assume the years are independent, so three good years in a row have probability 0.7 cubed, which is 0.343, for a skilled manager, and 0.5 cubed, which is 0.125, for an unskilled one. Your manager has just beaten the market three years in a row. How likely is it that they are skilled?
[[slnc 500]]
Click. Name the events: S is skilled, E is beats the market three years in a row. We know P of E given S equals 0.343, P of E given S complement equals 0.125, and P of S equals 0.1. We want P of S given E: exactly the reverse direction.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: gut feeling first: three wins in a row. How likely is it that this manager is skilled? Shout out a number.
[[slnc 800]]
Note for you: write a few guesses on the board. Many say well above 50 percent. Do not comment yet: the answer comes in two slides.

Total probability and Bayes' formula. Slide 27.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: a tree. From the root, two branches go to F and to F complement, with probabilities P of F and P of F complement; from each of them, two branches go to E and to E complement, with the conditional probabilities. The two leaves E are highlighted. Under the tree: multiply along a path; add the paths that end in E.
[[slnc 500]]
Bayes' formula: P of F given E equals P of E given F times P of F, over P of E. We know P of E given F, but we need P of F given E: Bayes' formula turns the condition around. Where does it come from? The numerator is P of E and F by the multiplication rule, so this is just the definition, P of F given E equals P of E and F over P of E, with the numerator written in a form we can compute.
[[slnc 500]]
The names: P of F is the prior, gold on the slide: what we believe before we see E. P of F given E is the posterior, teal on the slide: what we believe after we have seen E. Bayes is the rule for updating beliefs with evidence.
[[slnc 500]]
The denominator, P of E, comes from the law of total probability. E happens either together with F or together with F complement, never both. So E is cut into two pieces that do not overlap, E and F, and E and F complement, and P of E equals P of E and F plus P of E and F complement, which equals P of E given F times P of F, plus P of E given F complement times P of F complement. The second form uses the multiplication rule on each piece.
[[slnc 500]]
On the tree: multiply along a path; add the paths that end in E. That is the denominator. Bayes is then one path divided by all the paths ending in E. Written out in one line: P of F given E equals P of E given F times P of F, divided by the sum of P of E given F times P of F and P of E given F complement times P of F complement.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: for the fund manager, which path is the numerator, and which paths make the denominator?
[[slnc 800]]
Numerator: skilled, then three good years. Denominator: both paths that end in three good years, skilled and unskilled.

Skill or luck? The answer. Slide 28.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: this slide shows everything at once, like the solution of an exercise. Go through it top to bottom, then compare with the guesses on the board.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: the tree of the fund manager. From the root, S with 0.1 and S complement with 0.9; from S, E with 0.343 and E complement with 0.657; from S complement, E with 0.125 and E complement with 0.875. At the leaves, the path products 0.0343, 0.0657, 0.1125 and 0.7875; the two paths that end in E, 0.0343 and 0.1125, are highlighted.
[[slnc 500]]
Point at the tree. First split: skilled, 0.1, or not, 0.9. Then: three good years or not. Multiply along the paths: skilled and three good years, 0.1 times 0.343, which is 0.0343; unskilled and three good years, 0.9 times 0.125, which is 0.1125.
[[slnc 500]]
Total probability: two paths end in E, so P of E equals 0.0343 plus 0.1125, which is 0.1468. About 15 percent of all managers have three good years in a row.
[[slnc 400]]
Bayes: the skilled path over both paths. P of S given E equals P of E given S times P of S, over P of E, which is 0.0343 over 0.1468, approximately 0.234.

[[slnc 500]]
Why so low? Look at the two highlighted numbers. The lucky path, 0.1125, is more than three times the skilled path, 0.0343. Skill makes three good years almost three times as likely, 0.343 against 0.125, but unskilled managers are nine times as many. The large group wins.
[[slnc 400]]
With counts, if someone does not trust the formula: of 1000 managers, 100 are skilled, and about 34 of them have three good years; 900 are unskilled, and about 112 of them have three good years by luck. So about 146 stars, and only 34 of them are skilled.

Why Bayes' formula works: a picture. Slide 29.
[[slnc 600]]
Clock check: this slide is planned for minute 26. If you are more than about 5 minutes late here, shorten the picture proofs, on slides 29, 34 and 36, to their last row: what cancels, and what is left. The last row of this slide comes with a click. Point at the shapes at each step.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: at the top, a gray box with rounded corners, the sample space Omega. Inside it, a blue diamond, F, and a light blue circle, E. They overlap, and their overlap, F E, is red. Below, a row of four little fractions made of shapes. P of F is a small diamond over a small gray box. P of E is a small circle over a small gray box. P of F given E is the small red piece over a small circle. P of E given F is the small red piece over a small diamond. After a click, the last row: P of F given E equals P of E given F times P of F, over P of E, which equals red piece over diamond, times diamond over box, all over circle over box. The diamond below and the diamond above are crossed out in red, and so are the two boxes. What is left is the red piece over the circle, and that is P of F given E, by the definition.
[[slnc 500]]
Here is why Bayes' formula is true, in one picture. No numbers, only shapes.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 1: the picture. Point at the top. The gray box is the whole sample space Omega. The blue diamond is the hypothesis F, say, the manager is skilled. The light blue circle is the evidence E, three good years. Where they overlap, both happen: the red piece is F E.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 2: probability is a share. Point at the row of fractions. P of F is the share of the box that the diamond covers. Read the little fraction as diamond over box. In the same way, P of E is circle over box.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 3: conditional probability is a share inside what we know. Suppose we know that E happened. Then the outcome is somewhere in the circle, and the rest of the box no longer counts: the circle is our new box. Inside the circle, F happens exactly in the red piece. So P of F given E is red over circle. The other way around, if we know F happened, the diamond is the new box, and P of E given F is red over diamond. Point at the last two fractions of the row. Same red piece, different denominators: that is why P of E given F and P of F given E are different numbers.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: look at the picture. Which is bigger here, P of F given E, or P of E given F?
[[slnc 800]]
The red piece is the same; the circle is bigger than the diamond, so red over circle is smaller: in this picture, P of F given E is less than P of E given F. With a bigger diamond it would be the other way.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 4: plug the shapes into the formula. Click: the last row. Take the right-hand side of Bayes' formula and replace each probability by its fraction of shapes. The numerator, P of E given F times P of F, is red over diamond, times diamond over box. Point at the two diamonds: one is below, one is above, so they cancel. What is left is red over box, that is P of F E: the multiplication rule. The denominator, P of E, is circle over box.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 5: the boxes cancel. Point at the two crossed-out boxes: we divide red over box by circle over box, and the box drops out.
[[slnc 400]]
Step 6: read what is left. Red over circle. By step 3, that is exactly P of F given E, the share of the red piece in the circle. So the right-hand side equals P of F given E, and that is Bayes' formula.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board, if you want to write it: F E over F, times F over Omega, all over E over Omega, equals F E over E, the shapes standing for their sizes.
[[slnc 500]]
In one sentence: the red piece can be measured from two sides. From the diamond's side, P of F E equals P of F times P of E given F. From the circle's side, P of F E equals P of E times P of F given E. Same piece, so the two are equal; divide by P of E.
[[slnc 400]]
Keep this picture in mind for the next exercise. When the diamond is tiny, even a large share of it inside the circle is a small piece of the circle: P of E given F can be large while P of F given E stays small. That is what happened to the fund managers.

Why odds? Slide 32.
[[slnc 600]]
Clock check: this slide is planned for minute 39. If you are more than about 5 minutes late, skip part c of Exercise 9.14, on slide 39: say only that given no default, class C has probability about 0.19, almost the prior 0.2, then the lesson.
[[slnc 500]]
Why another way to write probabilities? First, because people in finance talk this way. Bettors and bookmakers quote 3 to 1 against; so do many traders. Credit scoring works with odds too: a scorecard adds points to the logarithm of the odds of default, one block of points for each characteristic of the borrower.
[[slnc 400]]
The main reason for us: with odds, updating becomes one multiplication. In Bayes' formula the denominator P of E needs total probability, the whole tree, again for every new piece of evidence. With odds, each new piece of evidence just multiplies the odds by one number, its likelihood ratio. So several signals are easy to combine.
[[slnc 500]]
Click. Back to our fund manager. After three good years, the probability of skill is 0.234. Now comes a fourth good year. With probabilities, we would redo the whole tree with 0.7 to the power 4 and 0.5 to the power 4. With odds, we multiply by one number: a good year is 0.7 over 0.5, that is 1.4 times as likely for a skilled manager. We do the multiplication in two slides.
[[slnc 1200]]

Odds. Slide 33.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: probability and odds as a pie, with the slice p, which is P of F, in navy, and the rest q, which is P of F complement, 1 minus p, in white. Probability is the navy slice over the whole pie, p over p plus q. Odds are the navy slice against the white rest, p to q.
[[slnc 500]]
The odds of F are P of F over P of F complement: how much more likely F is than its complement. Back: P of F equals the odds over 1 plus the odds.
[[slnc 400]]
Point at the pies. The probability compares the slice with the whole pie: p over p plus q, and p plus q is 1. The odds compare the slice with the rest: p to q. Same pie, two ways to read it.
[[slnc 400]]
Probability two thirds: odds two thirds over one third, which is 2, 2 to 1 in favor. Probability 0.2: odds 0.2 over 0.8, which is one quarter, 4 to 1 against. And back: odds one quarter give P equals one quarter over five quarters, which is 0.2.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: what are the odds of an event with probability one half?
[[slnc 800]]
1: even odds, 1 to 1.
[[slnc 400]]
Odds run from 0 to infinity; probability from 0 to 1. For small probabilities the two are almost the same: P equals 0.02 gives odds 0.02 over 0.98, approximately 0.0204.

Bayes' formula in odds form. Slide 34.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you: three steps, two clicks: the formula; click, the picture, explain what cancels; click, the fund manager in odds.
[[slnc 500]]
Bayes' formula in odds form: the new odds equal the old odds times the likelihood ratio. The new odds are also called the posterior odds: the odds of F after we have seen E. The old odds are the prior odds: before E. The likelihood ratio says how many times as likely the evidence is if F is true as if it is false.
[[slnc 400]]
If the evidence is more likely under F, the likelihood ratio is above 1 and the odds of F go up. If it is equally likely either way, the ratio is 1 and the evidence tells us nothing.
[[slnc 500]]
Why is it true? Write Bayes' formula for F and for F complement, with the same evidence E: P of F given E equals P of E given F times P of F, over P of E, and P of F complement given E equals P of E given F complement times P of F complement, over P of E. Divide the first by the second: the awkward denominator P of E cancels. That is the whole proof, and it is why the odds form is so quick: no total probability needed.
[[slnc 500]]

The generalized Bayes formula. Slide 35.
[[slnc 600]]

[[slnc 500]]
Now more than two hypotheses. F 1 to F n is a partition of Omega: mutually exclusive events, and exactly one of them occurs. Think of rating classes, or economic scenarios: boom, normal, recession. F and F complement is the special case n equals 2.
[[slnc 500]]
Bayes' formula is the same as before: P of F j given E equals P of E given F j times P of F j, over P of E. And the denominator again comes from total probability. The F i cut E into n pieces, E and F 1 up to E and F n, that do not overlap, so P of E equals the sum of P of E and F i, which equals the sum of P of E given F i times P of F i.
[[slnc 500]]
Point at the tree. The root now has n branches, one for each F i, and each is followed by a branch to E. The branches to E complement are left out. Multiply along a path to get P of F i times P of E given F i. Add all the paths to get P of E. Bayes is one path divided by all of them.
[[slnc 400]]
The posteriors, P of F 1 given E up to P of F n given E, add up to 1: given E, exactly one of the F i still occurs. A free check.

Independent events. Slide 40.
[[slnc 600]]
In every exercise so far, information changed the probability. Sometimes it does not. That is independence.
[[slnc 500]]
E and F are independent if P of E and F equals P of E times P of F. If P of F is greater than 0, this means P of E given F equals P of E: knowing F does not change the chances of E. To see it, divide the product rule by P of F: P of E given F equals P of E and F over P of F, which is P of E. Then E and F complement are independent too: if knowing F tells you nothing, knowing not F tells you nothing either.
[[slnc 500]]
For three events all four product rules must hold: the three pairs, and also P of E, F and G equals P of E times P of F times P of G. The pairs alone are not enough; Exercise 9.16, for self-study, shows three events that are independent in pairs but not all three together.
[[slnc 500]]
Two warnings. Mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent: if one occurs, the other cannot, so knowing one changes the other a lot. Indeed P of E and F is 0, but P of E times P of F is positive. And for n independent trials with success probability p, P of at least one success equals 1 minus the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n, because the complement, all n trials fail, has probability 1 minus p, to the power n, by the product rule.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny examples: two fair dice; first is a 6 and second is a 6 are independent, and the probability of both is one sixth times one sixth, which is 1 over 36. And independence can hold without any physical separation: one die, E is even, F is 1 or 2. Then E and F is just the outcome 2, and its probability, one sixth, equals one half times one third: independent. Check the product, do not trust intuition.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: are passed Math and passed Micro of Exercise 9.7 independent?
[[slnc 800]]
No: P of A times P of B is 0.72 times 0.64, approximately 0.461, not 0.54, which is P of A and B; and P of A given B, approximately 0.844, is not 0.72.

Discrete random variables. Slide 47.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: the graph of F for p of 1 equal to one quarter, p of 2 equal to one half, and p of 3 and p of 4 both equal to one eighth. The graph is a staircase: 0 up to 1, then steps up to one quarter at 1, three quarters at 2, seven eighths at 3, and 1 at 4, with a filled dot at the top of each jump, the value of F there, and an open dot at the bottom, the value just before the jump.
[[slnc 500]]
A random variable X assigns a number to every outcome. It is discrete if it takes finitely or countably many values: the number of defaults, the number of claims, a price on a grid. Its probability mass function, the pmf, is p of x equals P of X equals x; p of x is greater than or equal to 0, and the sum of p of x over all x is 1. The pmf is the complete list: which values, with which probabilities. The probabilities add up to 1 because exactly one of the values comes out.
[[slnc 500]]
The distribution function is F of x equals P of X less than or equal to x, which is the sum of p of y over all y less than or equal to x. For a discrete X it is a step function: it jumps by p of x at every value x, is constant in between, and rises from 0 to 1.
[[slnc 500]]
Read the graph from left to right. Below 1, X cannot be that small, so F is 0. At 1 it jumps by p of 1, one quarter; at 2 by one half, to three quarters; at 3 by one eighth, to seven eighths; at 4 by one eighth, to 1, and stays there. The height of each jump is the pmf at that point: the pmf and F carry the same information. The filled dots show that at a jump F takes the upper value: F of 2, P of X less than or equal to 2, includes the value 2 itself. The open dots are the values just before the jump.
[[slnc 400]]
On the board: P of a less than X less than or equal to b equals F of b minus F of a, and P of X greater than a equals 1 minus F of a.
[[slnc 400]]
Why? The event X less than or equal to b splits into X less than or equal to a, and a less than X less than or equal to b. They do not overlap, so their probabilities add; subtract F of a. And X greater than a is the complement of X less than or equal to a.
[[slnc 400]]
Example from the graph: P of 1 less than X less than or equal to 3 equals F of 3 minus F of 1, which is seven eighths minus one quarter, which is five eighths; that is p of 2 plus p of 3, one half plus one eighth. And P of X greater than 2 equals 1 minus F of 2, which is one quarter.
[[slnc 500]]
Ask the class: what is P of X less than 2?
[[slnc 800]]
F of 1, one quarter: less than 2 leaves the value 2 out.

Expected value, variance, standard deviation. Slide 48.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you. Clock check: this slide is planned for minute 7 of Session 2, minute 11 when you leave it. If you start it more than about 5 minutes late, on slide 50 read off only P of 4 less than S less than or equal to 9, which is F of 9 minus F of 4, two thirds, and skip the rest of that slide.
[[slnc 500]]
The expected value of X is the sum of x times p of x: the average of the values, weighted by their probabilities. If you repeat the experiment many times, the average of the observed values settles near the expected value. For a function, the expected value of g of X is the sum of g of x times p of x: apply g to each value, keep the probabilities. And the expected value of a X plus b equals a times the expected value of X, plus b: a change of units passes straight through, for example from euros to thousands of euros, or adding a fixed fee.
[[slnc 500]]
With mu equal to the expected value of X, the variance is the expected value of the square of X minus mu: the average squared distance from the mean. To compute it, use the shortcut: the expected value of X squared minus the square of the expected value of X, and take the square root for the standard deviation. The standard deviation is back in the units of X, so it is the number we interpret: a typical distance from the mean. And the variance of a X plus b equals a squared times the variance of X: adding a constant does not change the spread, and multiplying by a multiplies the standard deviation by the absolute value of a.
[[slnc 500]]
Why the shortcut? Expand the square: the expected value of the square of X minus mu is the expected value of X squared, minus 2 mu times the expected value of X, plus mu squared, which is the expected value of X squared minus mu squared.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example with the pmf of the last slide: the expected value of X is 1 times one quarter, plus 2 times one half, plus 3 times one eighth, plus 4 times one eighth, which is 2.125. The expected value of X squared is 1 times one quarter, plus 4 times one half, plus 9 times one eighth, plus 16 times one eighth, which is 5.375. So the variance of X is 5.375 minus 2.125 squared, approximately 0.859, and the standard deviation of X is approximately 0.927.
[[slnc 400]]
In finance: the expected value of X is the expected return or payoff, and the standard deviation of X measures the risk.

Exercise 9.21. The payoff of a call option, a finance example. Slides 51 and 52.
[[slnc 600]]
Finance context: a call option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy a stock at a fixed price, the strike price K, on a given date. If the stock price S on that date is above K, the holder buys at K and gains S minus K; otherwise the option is simply not used and pays nothing. So the payoff is the maximum of S minus K and 0.
[[slnc 500]]

Bernoulli distribution. Slide 53.
[[slnc 600]]
Now the named distributions. Each one comes with a story; if the story fits the problem, the formulas are ready to use. The simplest story first.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: a spinner with a gold sector of share p, labeled one spin: success with probability p; and next to it the two bars of the pmf for p equals 0.3: height 0.7 at 0, failure, and 0.3 at 1, success.
[[slnc 500]]
A Bernoulli variable is a yes-or-no question turned into a number: 1 for yes, 0 for no. Think of the spinner: it stops in the gold sector with probability p. Its mean is 1 times p, plus 0 times 1 minus p, which is p. For the variance: X is 0 or 1, so X squared equals X, and the expected value of X squared is p; then the variance of X is p minus p squared, which is p times the quantity 1 minus p.
[[slnc 500]]
The key sentence: the expected value of an indicator is the probability of its event. Counting with indicators is how we get the binomial mean on the next slide.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example: a loan defaults with probability 0.05; X equals 1 if it defaults. The expected value of X is 0.05, and the variance of X is 0.05 times 0.95, which is 0.0475.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: for which p is the variance largest?
[[slnc 800]]
For p equals one half: then p times 1 minus p is one quarter. At p equals 0 or p equals 1 there is no uncertainty, and the variance is 0.

Binomial distribution. Slide 54.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: a row of ten trials, F, S, F, F, S, F, F, F, S, F, with the three successes in gold, and the caption: one order with i equals 3, probability p cubed times 1 minus p to the power 7, and 10 choose 3 such orders. Below it, the bars of P of X equals i for n equals 10 and p equals 0.3: highest at i equals 3, in gold, and almost nothing above 7.
[[slnc 500]]
The binomial story: n independent trials, each a success with the same probability p; X counts the successes. Why this formula? Look at the row of ten trials on the slide. That one order, with i successes and n minus i failures, has probability p to the power i, times the quantity 1 minus p, to the power n minus i: by independence we multiply. There are n choose i such orders, one for each choice of the i successful trials, and they are mutually exclusive, so we add. In the picture: 10 choose 3, which is 120 orders, each with probability 0.3 cubed times 0.7 to the power 7, so P of X equals 3 is approximately 0.267, the gold bar.
[[slnc 500]]
Why n p and n p times 1 minus p? X is the sum of X 1 up to X n, where X k is the Bernoulli indicator of a success on trial k. Expected values add, so the expected value of X is n p; for independent trials variances add too, so the variance of X is n p times the quantity 1 minus p. And with n equals 1 we are back at the Bernoulli distribution. In the picture, the expected value of X is 10 times 0.3, which is 3: the bars peak at the mean.
[[slnc 500]]
Tiny example: three fair coin flips, X is the number of heads. P of X equals 2 equals 3 choose 2, times 0.5 squared, times 0.5, which is 3 over 8: the three orders H H T, H T H and T H H, each with probability one eighth. And the expected value of X is 3 times 0.5, which is 1.5.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: what are the three conditions to check before using the binomial?
[[slnc 800]]
A fixed number of trials; independent; the same p every time.

Poisson distribution. Slide 56.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you. Clock check: this slide is planned for minute 39 of Session 2. If you start it more than about 5 minutes late, do part e of Exercise 9.26, on slide 60, in one sentence: after five failed calls the waiting starts afresh, P of X greater than 8 given X greater than 5 equals P of X greater than 3, which is 0.512; this is the memoryless property.
[[slnc 500]]
On the slide: a time line of one day, from 0:00 to 24:00, with claims arriving at random times, here 3 of them, in gold. Below it, the bars of P of X equals i for lambda equals 3: equally high at 2 and 3, the bar at 3 in gold, then falling off to the right.
[[slnc 500]]
The Poisson story: the number of rare events in a fixed period: claims a day, defaults a year, errors on a page. The events come one by one, at random times, independently of each other, as on the time line of the slide. With parameter lambda greater than 0, P of X equals i equals e to the minus lambda, times lambda to the power i, over i factorial, for i equal to 0, 1, 2, and so on; and the expected value and the variance of X are both lambda: lambda is the average number of events in the period. One number describes the whole distribution.
[[slnc 500]]
Note that X has no upper limit: any count 0, 1, 2, and so on is possible, but large counts are very unlikely, because i factorial grows fast. The factor e to the minus lambda makes the probabilities add up to 1, since the sum of lambda to the power i over i factorial is e to the lambda.
[[slnc 400]]
Ask the class: look at the chart. Why are the bars at 2 and 3 equally high?
[[slnc 800]]
Going from i minus 1 to i multiplies the probability by lambda over i; at i equals 3 that is 3 over 3, which is 1. So the bars rise while i is less than lambda and fall after: the peak is at lambda.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: a bank sees on average 2 defaults a year in a loan book, modeled as Poisson with lambda equals 2. No default in a year: e to the minus 2, approximately 0.135; exactly one: 2 times e to the minus 2, approximately 0.271.

Geometric distribution. Slide 58.
[[slnc 600]]
Note for you. Clock check: this slide is planned for minute 48 of Session 2. If you start it more than about 5 minutes late, in part d of Exercise 9.28, on slide 62, give only P of X equals 2, 3360 over 15504, approximately 0.2167.

The geometric story: independent trials with success probability p; X is the number of the trial on which the first success happens. [[slnc 400]]

[[slnc 400]]

Hypergeometric distribution. Slide 61.
[[slnc 600]]
On the slide: an urn with 20 balls, 4 of them special, in gold. Five places in the urn are dashed: those balls were picked and not put back. An arrow, pick n equals 5, leads to the sample of five balls, one of them gold: X equals 1.
[[slnc 500]]
The hypergeometric story: from capital N items, m of which are special, we pick n at random without replacement; X counts the special ones picked. In the picture: 20 balls, 4 gold; we take out 5, and the sample happens to contain one gold ball. The formula is favorable over all, with last week's counting: choose i of the m special items and n minus i of the capital N minus m others, out of all ways to choose n of capital N.
[[slnc 500]]
Without replacement the picks are not independent, so X is not binomial: each special item drawn makes the next one less likely. If the first ball is gold, only 3 of the remaining 19 are gold. If capital N is large compared with n, removing a few items hardly matters, and it is close to the binomial with parameters n and m over capital N.
[[slnc 500]]
The mean, n times m over capital N, is the same as for the binomial with p equal to m over capital N: a share m over capital N of the items is special, and we pick n of them.
[[slnc 400]]
Tiny example: an urn with 5 balls, 2 of them red; pick 2. Exactly one red: 2 choose 1 times 3 choose 1, over 5 choose 2, which is 6 over 10, which is 0.6.
